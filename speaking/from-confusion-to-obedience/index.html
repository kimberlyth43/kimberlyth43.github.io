Confusion Turns to Obedience

Main Point

God turns his people’s confusion into obedience by correcting them, restraining them, and leading them to walk in his ways.

Introduction

Hook

Hiroo Onoda was a Japanese lieutenant stationed on Lubang Island in early 1945. Eventually he found himself alone. Later that same year the war ended. Leaflets were dropped, newspapers were left behind, and search parties came looking for him. But he dismissed all of it as enemy propaganda designed to trick him. He did not believe the war was truly over until March 1974, when his former commanding officer flew to the island and personally relieved him of duty. He had been confused. And because he was confused, he fought a war that had already ended—for twenty-nine years.

My point is that you can pay a very high price for being confused. But what a blessing it is to be corrected so that you can turn away from the path of confusion. I am glad, therefore, that God has given us Nehemiah 8:9–18, because it shows us how confusion turns to obedience.

First, let me review where we are in the story of Ezra–Nehemiah and in the larger story of the Bible.

Review

The Old Testament shows us a sad, repeating cycle in which God’s people abandon and betray him.1 They live under the curse—Adam and Eve banished from the garden, Israel enslaved in Egypt, Israel exiled in Babylon. God rescues them. He convicts them so that they seem to repent and believe. Yet they abandon him again.

By abandoning God they show that they do not actually love him with all their heart, soul, mind, and strength (Deut. 6; Mark 12:30) as he deserves. That is the problem of humanity the Old Testament reveals: we refuse to love God from the heart.

God can meet us on the mountain, speak to us audibly, go before us as a pillar of fire, give us a temple in which to meet him, and provide the most specific instructions imaginable—yet humanity still does not want him. If we did, we would not abandon him the way Israel did over and over.

We prefer to worship gods we have made, so that we can control them and behave as we please. That is why the cults of old were so grotesque; they satisfied the worst human desires. In the end, the natural human heart worships itself—its own comfort, its own control, its own wants, its own dreams—and follows God only insofar as he does not contradict those desires.

The entire Old Testament is showing us that humanity has a heart problem. We are spiritually dead. You can give the people back the temple, rebuild Jerusalem, reread the law, and bring them back from Babylon, but Babylon is still in their hearts.

This is the story we see unfolding in Ezra–Nehemiah.2 Zerubbabel leads God’s people back to the land to rebuild the temple at Cyrus’s decree. Though the work is opposed, God protects them and they finish the temple. Ezra returns with more exiles at the command of Artaxerxes. He helps the people obey the law and turn from the sin of compromise through intermarriage. Nehemiah returns at Artaxerxes’s command, rebuilds the wall amid great opposition, and keeps a sober mind as he fights God’s enemies. After the wall is rebuilt, he turns his attention to building up the people and repopulating Jerusalem. That is the section we are in now.

First Nehemiah updated the genealogy of those who had returned so he would know who was in the land. Then he and Ezra organized a gathering of all the people. God first moved them from ignorance to understanding: they gathered before him, revered his name, heard his word read, and were taught what it meant. That brought understanding about their sin, and they were convicted. But they were still confused. They did not know what God wanted them to do. So in Nehemiah 8:9–18 God moves his people from confusion to obedience by correcting, restraining, and leading them.

Let us begin by looking at how God uses correction.

Correcting

After the people come to understanding about their sin in verses 1–8, they are convicted and ready to mourn and weep (v. 9). To us that sounds like an entirely appropriate reaction, and mourning over sin is appropriate. But notice what Nehemiah, Ezra, and the Levites say: “This day is holy to the Lord your God; do not mourn or weep.”

This should stop us in our tracks. We would never imagine spiritual leaders shutting down mourning and weeping over sin. To understand what is happening, we need to answer three questions: First, why do the leaders correct the people? Second, why is their correction so surprising to us? Third, why is correction crucial to remedying confusion?

1. Why do the leaders correct the people? Nehemiah, Ezra, and the teachers correct them and tell them not to mourn and weep because, in this case, mourning and weeping would have added to their sin.

They are celebrating the Feast of Trumpets, which comes just before the Feast of Booths. In Numbers 10:10 God commands, “On the day of your gladness also, and at your appointed feasts and at the beginnings of your months, you shall blow the trumpets over your burnt offerings and over the sacrifices of your peace offerings. They shall be a reminder of you before your God: I am the Lord your God.”

That is what the leaders mean when they say, “This day is holy to the Lord your God.” They are not saying it is never appropriate to mourn before God. They are saying, “God himself has set this day apart for rejoicing, so that is what we are going to do.” By correcting the people, their leaders save them from adding to their sin.

What is astonishing is that the people had the best of intentions in their mourning and weeping. How could it get any better than mourning over your sin? Yet the leaders still correct them. What we see is this: worshiping God in the way he says he wants to be worshiped is more important than worshiping God the way we feel like worshiping him. Even if our hearts feel “right,” God should be worshiped the way he has commanded. Worship is a matter of the heart, just as Jesus taught us. There is no true worship that is not heartfelt. But that does not give us permission to worship however we please.

Right worship fits itself to what it ought to feel and is not driven primarily by passion. Here we see religious fervor arise among the Israelites. Rather than fan that fervor into flame, the leaders call them to change direction entirely. They must put out the flame of passion and restrain themselves.

Psalm-singing, which Pastor Andy preached on last week, is one obvious example. Refusing to sing the Psalms is not acceptable, even if your heart is full of praise while you sing something else. This matters because it runs directly against the way we naturally think as modern individualists. We believe “it’s the thought that counts.” But our thoughts are not that important. Even our best thoughts—like the Israelites weeping over sin—are subject to correction if they are not in line with what God has commanded.

So we must be ready and open for correction, willing for even our best intentions to be corrected and set on the path God has prescribed. We cannot assume that because something feels right to us, it is right. That would be leaning on our own understanding. God commands us instead to acknowledge him in all our ways, which means that the moment we are corrected we change course and go the way he is showing us.

The leaders correct the people in order to save them from increasing their sin.

2. Why is their correction so surprising to us? We find it almost unimaginable that someone would say, “Now is not the time to be grieved about your sin. You should be happy instead.” Imagine a church gathering in which people are struck by their sin and the pastors say, “Stop weeping and mourning. It is not time for that right now.”

Why does this strike us as unimaginable, even inappropriate? Because we have internalized the idea that “real spirituality” is serious, somber, and even a little sad all the time. If that sounds like an overstatement, ask yourself which of these two reactions sounds more spiritual: tearing your clothes and lying in sackcloth and ashes, or eating rich food, drinking sweet wine, dancing, and singing? Why does the sackcloth sound more spiritual to us?

I suspect the reason varies somewhat for each of us, but many of us share this: we do not yet believe that God is as good a Father as he actually is. He delights in giving good gifts. He likes to see us rejoice. He likes to see us play, to see us smile, to hear us laugh, to watch us eat and drink. That is why he even commands it.

Think how radical this is. It would have been wrong for the people to keep mourning their sin instead of feasting and rejoicing. God wants feasting and rejoicing so much that he commands it, and that command overrides the desire to mourn and weep before him.

This is what Nehemiah means when he says, “Do not be grieved, for the joy of the Lord is your strength.” The phrase “joy of the Lord” could mean your joy in the Lord or the Lord’s joy in his people. The syntax does not decide the matter with certainty, but it does not matter much, because both are gloriously true. They are not to grieve because they are upheld and strengthened by their joy in the God who takes joy in rescuing and redeeming them.

Apply this to one area of life together: what do we do when we have sinned? Some of us are tempted toward morbid introspection. We pout, grieve, and beat ourselves up, and we even feel spiritual for it. After all, we care more about our sin than those other people. But pouting and scowling is not what God tells us to do with our sin. It is not holy or impressive, because it is not God’s way. We are not supposed to wallow in sorrow. We are supposed to receive God’s forgiveness and then believe we are forgiven. No amount of sackcloth and ashes will set us right with God. But Jesus will. And if he has, we can stop pouting as though we are still on the outs with God. If you have not repented, you are on the outs with God—repent. But if you have confessed your sin, then receive forgiveness. God has separated you from the penalty of your sin as far as the east is from the west. He has removed your guilt and shame. So believe that he has removed them instead of trying to wallow in them.

Wallowing in the self-pity of introspective guilt is what it looks like not to believe you are forgiven. Turn from the sin of unbelief and believe. Do not deceive yourself into thinking you are especially spiritual because sin is such a big deal to you. If you want to be spiritual, deal with sin God’s way: confess, repent, trust, and obey.

Not only does God smile upon our normal day-to-day eating and drinking; sometimes he commands his people to pull out all the stops and celebrate with a feast. We understand this intuitively with earthly celebrations like birthdays. We have special desserts, special traditions, candles, and parties. We do the same with baby showers and bridal showers, and it is good and right. We demonstrate our gladness in God by slicing into sweet cakes, laying out beautiful fruit trays, uncorking the best wine, and grilling expensive steaks. It is not only not wrong; it is right. It is what you must do.

There is a story Pastor Andy told me while I was in seminary. D. A. Carson was counseling a couple who had come to see why complementarianism is crucial—that a man must lead his wife. They said, “We see it in Scripture, but we don’t like it yet.” Carson replied, “Well, you’re halfway there. It’s not enough to see it. You need to learn to love it.” The same is true here. It is not enough to see that rejoicing with food and drink, dancing and music, is sometimes acceptable. We must see that it is appropriate, right, and commanded. We must love it. That is one reason I am excited it will be part of our upcoming conference. We come together to consider how Christ is King of our homes. It is only fitting that we also dance and rejoice that Christ is King of our homes.

Overall, do not be surprised that God requires rejoicing. Do not be surprised that he sets aside days specifically for celebration. That is the kind of God he is. Take this passage as a warning not to try to be more spiritual than God. Do not add to your sin by avoiding celebration and rejoicing and exultation with gratitude in your heart for what God has done, as though he would rather his children always be sad, serious, and somber. The apostle Paul was right when he said, “Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, rejoice.”

3. Why is correction crucial to remedying confusion? Notice that left to themselves the people would have gone their own way. This is the glory of being corrected. Though you are heading in the wrong direction, you can be shown the right way and course-correct. You are not stuck eternally on the wrong path. You can repent and change, but in order to repent and change you must be corrected—which, of course, requires humility.

We must be people who, when confronted with our failings, do not strike a defensive, know-it-all posture as though we are above correction and the world should conform to us. Rather we should conform ourselves to Christ, and the way we discover where we need to conform is to be corrected by God’s word.

This is one reason our church has made it a priority to proclaim God’s word. We want to be constantly corrected by it.

The first means God uses to move his people from confusion to obedience is correction. The leaders correct the people so they will not add to their sin by ignoring God’s command to rejoice. We are surprised because God’s lavish goodness has not yet sunk in. So even now let your heart be corrected. Do not try to be more spiritual than God. Learn to love what God loves—and that includes delighted rejoicing.

Restraining

The second means God uses is restraint. The people restrain their feelings and redirect them in the way God commands.

Verses 11–12 tell us, “So the Levites calmed all the people, saying, ‘Be quiet, for this day is holy; do not be grieved.’ And all the people went their way to eat and drink and to send portions and to make great rejoicing, because they had understood the words that were declared to them.” It is hard for us in our hyper-emotional age to imagine a group of people being corrected by the truth. But they heard the truth from the Levites, understood it, and changed their hearts and minds accordingly. Rather than give in to religious fervor and hand themselves over to their passions, the leaders commanded them to get control of themselves, to restrain themselves, and to do what God asked rather than what they felt like doing.

Pastor Andy has been teaching us what it means to worship God in the most fitting way we know how. We follow a version of the regulative principle that says Scripture must warrant everything we do. In other words, worshiping God is not a “buffet-it-your-way” situation. We obey God and do exactly what he says. That is true of our passions and affections as well. In this case the people were taught, they were corrected, and on the basis of their new understanding they changed the feelings of their hearts. Instead of continuing in the fervor of grief, they turned to rejoicing.

God had told them how they were to come before him: rejoicing. So the leaders said, “No, no—put the weeping away. Now is the time for rejoicing. That is what this day is set apart for.” The people obeyed because they understood that if they gave in to their passions and did what they wanted—weep and mourn—they would only be adding to their disobedience.

We have already seen the correction and change of perspective this requires. We must abandon the notion that God is suspicious of rejoicing, like the White Witch angry at feasting and enjoyment. That is not God. But there is another great challenge for us in this passage.

Consider the amount of restraint, discipline, and self-control God is both asking for and enabling in his people. God’s people are not to be blown to and fro by their moods or attitudes. If this is true in how we come to worship God, how much more is it true in the rest of life?

The people had such strong feelings that they were weeping, yet God still expected them to get control of those feelings, redirect them, and move from weeping to rejoicing. Brothers and sisters, your mood ought not govern you.

When the kitchen is hot and you still need to make dinner, your mood ought not control you. When your toddlers will not sit still, your mood ought not control you. Instead we must exercise self-control and restrain ourselves so that we rightly worship God—both as the gathered people of God and as the scattered people of God.

This is the opposite of “following your heart.” Instead of following their hearts, they had to learn to follow what is right.

Remember, this is what it means to be sober-minded, and Nehemiah has been an outstanding example to us. He has been sober-minded in the face of trials from enemies both outside and inside the community. The result of his sober-mindedness, his emotional self-control, his ability to rein in his feelings, has been that he has effectively led the people to reestablish the city. Now we see that God does not ask this only of leaders. He asks it of all his people. We must be able to rein in our feelings, control our moods, and change our attitudes so that when we have the wrong attitude before God we receive correction, restrain ourselves, and go the way he lays out for us.

This is easily applicable. Kids, when you get in a bad mood and nothing seems to be going your way, God expects you—and he will help you—to change your attitude. Instead of being discontent and grouchy, you can become sweet and a delight to be with. Moms, when your children have sinned and disobeyed repeatedly and it has left you frustrated, but now you have forgiven them and it is time to move on, you can turn from that underlying anger. With God’s help, replace it with joy. Men, when you are at work and simply not in the mood to work your hardest, with God’s help you can turn from a lazy attitude and replace it with discipline and ambition. Everyone, when you arrive at our worship service and do not feel like singing, praising, or listening to God’s word, you can—with God’s help—replace that irreverent attitude with the fear of God.

One of the great gifts and commands God is giving his people is self-control. We are to possess ourselves; we are to be sober-minded. And that is exactly what God’s people do in this passage. In verse 12, having understood the words declared to them, they go and obey. They eat and drink, send portions to those who have nothing prepared, and make great rejoicing. They do not persist in the way that seemed right to them. God gives them agile hearts so they can move from weeping to rejoicing at his command.

The first means God uses to move his people from confusion to obedience is correction. The second is restraint. Rather than give in to the fervor of their passions, they exercise self-control and change their hearts on the basis of the truth explained to them.

Leading

The third means God uses to bring his people from confusion to obedience is their appointed leaders.

After the people understand they should not give in to weeping, the heads of households gather with Ezra to study the words of the law more deeply so they will know what they should do. In other words, the people who ought to be leading stood up, took responsibility, and led. They studied the Book of the Law until they understood what they were supposed to do.

Ezra gathers the leaders, the heads of households, to instruct them and show them from the Bible what they ought to do. Notice that Ezra does not merely tell them; they find it written in the Law of the Lord (v. 14). He opens the book and shows them exactly what to do: they are to dwell in booths and feast. Now all the people whose job it is to make sure this happens are gathered to learn that they must proclaim and publish this in all their towns.

It is not a coincidence that Ezra and Nehemiah accomplish bringing the people to obedience through the heads of households. This is the way God plans to work in the world. Israel had a more complicated structure for heads of households than we do, but the basic principle remains. God works in the world through covenant heads.

In fact, it is by working through covenant headship that you and I are saved. We are counted in our covenant head, Christ, so that his righteousness is counted as ours. God provides for us the blessings of Christ, who represents us before God, just as before we were under the curse of Adam, who had been our covenant head and representative. God still plans to lead the world through covenant heads, including covenant heads under Christ. That especially means God plans to work in households through husbands, because they are the head of the household. It is through husbands that families are changed so that they obey God and walk in his ways.

Ezra, the high priest and covenant head of the people, gathered the other covenant heads and showed them, “This is what God’s word says.” So they did it as they brought that word to their households. That is what we need to be able to do. It is why our church makes it a priority to train and equip men. It is one reason Pastor Andy decided to teach the theology classes that are coming. It is why the pastors make themselves available at nine o’clock every Sunday morning. It is why we make it a priority to be with you on Wednesday nights. It is why we have made it a priority to meet with you in your homes, to understand what is going on in your vocations and family life. The truth is that when you win the men, you win their families.

The heads of households in Nehemiah 8 do exactly what they should. After reading in the Book of the Law what they ought to do, they proclaim it to the people and make sure it happens. You will notice that verse 17 (in the ESV) says, “All the assembly of those who had returned from the captivity made booths and lived in the booths, for from the days of Jeshua the son of Nun to that day the people of Israel had not done so.” That makes it sound as though Israel had never celebrated the Feast of Booths since Joshua’s conquest, but we know that is not true because they celebrated it in Ezra 3. The ESV leaves ambiguous what the Hebrew leaves ambiguous. The CSB captures the meaning well: they had not celebrated like this since the days of Joshua. The text is saying that these heads of households, once they understood what was supposed to happen, led the people in a celebration of the Feast of Booths that was second only to the days of Joshua, when the people had just come out of Egypt.

I have sometimes been in church or conference settings where someone holds up a very small, thin book and says, “Now here’s a man-sized book,” and everyone laughs. I was talking recently with a pastor whose congregation includes men who say they love the idea of biblical patriarchy and leading their households, yet they complain about reading a book instead of listening to a podcast or article. That kind of anti-intellectualism is simply incompatible with the calling. Being the head of your house means you must know the way to lead your family. It does not mean you must be an academic. It does not mean you must read every book in existence or be an expert on every topic. But it does mean you must be able to share with your family where to go according to the Book of the Law, just as Ezra did for the heads of households.

Husbands and fathers, if you do not already, consider it your job to proclaim to your household how they ought to glorify God in all of life. It is our job to instruct them how to be living sacrifices, how to give themselves to the service of God in everyday life. And this especially means pointing them to believe and trust in the King of kings in every circumstance.

A key part of the Feast of Booths was a water offering. It was on that day that Jesus stood up and cried out, “If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, ‘Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.’” He was speaking about the Spirit, whom those who believed in him were to receive.

Heads of households, this is the most important news for you to proclaim to your families and to everyone you have influence over. Go to the thirsty and invite them to come to Jesus and drink. Teach them to receive the body and blood of our Lord, broken and poured out for them. Teach them to rejoice because the joy of the Lord is their strength. Teach them to confess their sins and get right with God. Teach them to be correctable. Imitate your good Shepherd and lead them beside streams of living water.

The first means God uses is correction. The second is restraint. And the third is the leaders God has appointed. Through all three, God brings about obedience.

Result: Obedience

After receiving God’s grace in correction, restraint, and godly leadership, the people obey. Verses 17–18 are wonderfully matter-of-fact about it. They just do it. They just obey. Let us learn from this simplicity. It seems so complicated to get a people who do not understand the feast to keep it. But it is not complicated. Just show them what God wants and call them to obey.

Do not make obedience complicated. David Mathis reminds us that we receive the gift of effort by doing. Just do it. Beware the temptation to think and plan and dream about obeying without ever starting. This week I finished a short book called Sir Knight of the Splendid Way. It is written in the same allegorical style as Pilgrim’s Progress. I recommend it to you, especially to you men.

At one point Sir Constant, the story’s main character, comes to a city called Dangerous. In that city he does not find lewd women or stacks of money or many of the other things we usually think of as dangerous. He finds a city full of peace and rest. Those who stay fall under a spell and dream of great and noble deeds, but they do not know they are dreaming. They forget they are only dreaming, and their hours and years fly by.

He meets Sir Ardent, who gazes into the horizon dreaming of himself entering the great city with shouts of joy and acclamation. Everyone is impressed by all the great deeds he has done. Sir Constant must tell him, “There is no city, there is no knight. Dry and barren is the plain, barren are thy dreams. There is no glory for thee, for thou art but a foolish dreamer with rusted arms. Awake, and come with me.” Do not allow yourself to dream, to plan, to try for obedience. Simply trust and obey.

Conclusion

Nehemiah 8 gives us a picture of how God’s people move from ignorance about their sin to understanding, and then from confusion to obedience. To move them from ignorance to understanding, God gathers them together. He works with people as covenantal groups. They are able to receive God’s word because they revere him, and they show it by their actions and their words. They read God’s word and so are ruled by him. And they teach what the words mean so that the people understand. But although they understand that they have sinned according to God’s law, they are still confused about what to do. They want to mourn and weep on a day God has set apart for rejoicing.

So to move his people from confusion to obedience, God corrects them through the teaching of his word. He gives them grace to restrain themselves so that, rather than follow their hearts, they follow what is right. Then he works through the appointed leaders to show the people how to obey, and the people obey.

The common denominator in moving away from ignorance and confusion is God’s word. God’s word teaches us so that we gain understanding, and God’s word corrects us so that we follow what is right. So keep gathering with God’s people to receive his word, and be humble enough to change as it shows you the right way to go.

Just as the story of the whole Bible teaches us, our only hope is for God to give us new hearts. And in the new covenant he has done exactly that. We do not have to descend into a cycle of sin and rebellion. We can joyfully obey our God because he has made us new. So let us walk in that new way—abandoning our confusion, being corrected by God’s word, restraining our passions, and being led by those God gives us. Amen.
