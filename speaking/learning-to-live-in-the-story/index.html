Learning to Live in the Story of Ezra–Nehemiah

Ezra–Nehemiah · Series Introduction

Preached by Nathan Colestock — February 9, 2025

Christ the King Church · Stillwater, MN

Learning to Live in the Story: An Introduction to Ezra and Nehemiah

Please pray with me. Heavenly Father, may the words of my mouth and the meditations of our hearts be acceptable in your sight, our great God and our Redeemer. Amen.

Series Introduction and Pastoral Context

This morning we are not continuing in Andy’s series in First Timothy. Instead, we are beginning a new series in Ezra and Nehemiah. Andy and I made this decision together because we believe it will serve you, the congregation, more effectively. It gives greater continuity within the series, since the same preacher will carry the whole book from beginning to end. It also gives us as preachers helpful flexibility to adjust the pace of the text even the week before without disrupting the other preacher’s preparation.

Perhaps you are wondering how often each of your pastors will preach this year. Andy will preach about seventy-five percent of the time. I will preach about twelve times. Tom will preach three times, and Dustin will preach once. The purpose of this sermon is to introduce the series in Ezra and Nehemiah. I do not have one specific text to unfold. Instead, I want to lay the whole story before us so that we can see it clearly from beginning to end.

The Historical Context and the Repeating Pattern of Scripture

I hope that Nehemiah chapter 9, which we just heard, has helped place the context of this story back in your minds. You remember that the people of God were enslaved in Egypt. God called them out in the exodus, sending plagues on the Egyptians, bringing his people out with a mighty hand through the Red Sea, and destroying their enemies. But the first generation of Israelites lacked the faith to enter the promised land, so they wandered in the wilderness until the next generation arose. God raised up Joshua to lead them in the conquest of Canaan. They took the land and settled in it.

Then came the period of the judges and the kings—of Saul, David, and Solomon. After Solomon’s days, the kingdom split in two because of his unfaithfulness, becoming the northern kingdom and the southern kingdom. Their kings grew worse and worse until God sent both kingdoms into exile: first the northern kingdom to Assyria, and later the southern kingdom to Babylon. You can read in Daniel how the kingdom of Babylon eventually fell to the kingdom of Persia after the pride and faithlessness of its king. That is exactly where we find ourselves at the beginning of Ezra—the people of God are in exile under Persia, and God is preparing to bring them back to the land according to his word.

I wonder if you noticed the repeating pattern of Scripture as we heard Nehemiah 9, or if you have seen it in your own reading of the Old Testament. The pattern begins with a gracious decree from God that his kingdom should be built. He will gather his people and establish his rule. Then he supplies everything they need. He calls them as his people at Mount Sinai and gives them his word. He calls them to live in the land and gives them the strength to take it.

Next, God’s enemies attack. Pharaoh pursues them with chariots at the Red Sea. The Edomites refuse them passage. The Canaanites rise up against them as they seek to possess the land. But when God’s people are attacked, God fights for them, and they win. The Red Sea crashes down on Pharaoh’s army. The promised land is taken. The walls of Jericho fall. God’s people are victorious.

In the grace of that victory, they consecrate themselves to God. They make or renew a covenant with him—as at Mount Sinai, at the end of Deuteronomy before entering the land, or at the end of Joshua after the conquest. Then God blesses them because of their faith as they have renewed covenant with him. Finally, the cycle ends as God’s people, fat on the provision he has given, forget him, turn away, and act faithlessly.

Ezra–Nehemiah—which is actually one book in two scrolls, which is why you hear me say “Ezra–Nehemiah” rather than two separate books—tells this same repeating tragedy twice. It is the tragedy of a God so gracious that he runs after his prodigal son and receives back his wayward bride again and again, yet the faith of the fathers never takes root in the sons. It never lasts more than a generation or two before they return to other gods.

I want you to see this narrative cycle in Ezra and Nehemiah. So please open your Bibles to Ezra chapter 1, and watch the story unfold across the pages before you. This is crucial if we are to understand how similar their story is to the chapter of God’s story we are living in today.

In Ezra chapter 1, God’s gracious decree comes in verse 1. It comes through Cyrus, whose spirit God stirs so that he sends the people back from Persia to rebuild the temple and the city. God provides everything they need. In verse 5, Cyrus gives them an expense account so they lack nothing for the work. When they arrive and begin rebuilding, God’s enemies attack. Turn to chapter 4, verse 1. At first the enemies try to join the project in order to compromise it from within. When that fails, they bribe officials and write accusing letters to the king so that the work stops. It looks as though this reformation will fail because the king’s favor has been withdrawn.

But God fights for his people. In chapter 5, he sends the prophets Haggai and Zechariah to speak the word of the Lord, and the people begin building again. Their enemies appeal once more, this time to King Darius. Yet when Darius examines the matter, he not only permits them to continue, he renews their expense account from the royal treasury and threatens anyone who would hinder the work. God’s people are victorious because God fights for them. So they consecrate themselves to God. In chapter 6, verse 19, they celebrate the Passover. In response to their faithfulness, God richly blesses them. In chapter 7, he sends Ezra to teach the law, along with many others who return as reinforcements bearing gifts for the temple. Yet no sooner does Ezra arrive than he discovers that the people have once again rejected God. In chapter 9, they have intermarried with the peoples of the land. The cycle completes its tragic arc as God’s people, sated by his blessings, forget him and go their own way.

The book of Nehemiah begins the cycle again. There is another decree from God, this time in chapter 2 through Artaxerxes. Nehemiah asks the king for permission to return and help rebuild. God stirs the king’s heart so that he grants it. God also provides what they need. In chapter 2, verse 8, Artaxerxes opens the royal forests so they have all the timber required. When they arrive, God’s people are attacked again. In chapter 4, verse 1, there is a plot to destroy them by military force. In chapter 5, verse 1, enemies arise from within: wealthy Israelites who are oppressing their brothers and sisters, even forcing some to sell their daughters into slavery to pay debts. In chapter 6, false prophets have been bribed to discredit Nehemiah.

Yet despite all these attacks, God fights for his people and they prevail. In chapter 6, verse 15, the wall is finished. They consecrate themselves to God. At the end of chapter 7, they bring rich gifts for the worship of the temple. In chapter 8, they read the law. In chapter 9, they confess their sins. In chapter 10, they make a solemn covenant: “It will be different this time, O God. Our fathers were stubborn, but we will not be. We will provide for the temple. We will honor your Sabbath.”

Then Nehemiah returns home when the king grants him leave. When he comes back in chapter 13, what does he find? God’s people have rejected the Lord once more. They have allowed Tobiah the Ammonite, their enemy, to live in the temple courts. They have failed to give the Levites their portions, so the temple is no longer functioning. They are profaning the Sabbath, allowing foreigners to trade on it, and they have intermarried with the peoples of the land again. Ezra–Nehemiah is a double tragedy that ends where it began. It is the same story the Old Testament has told over and over: a gracious God who redeems his people, and a stubborn people who will not turn to him.

Receiving the Bible as a Story

This is the story that, in our series through Ezra and Nehemiah, we must learn to receive from God. It is vital that we understand we are receiving a story. Much of the Bible consists of propositional truth—clear doctrinal statements connected by logic—and those portions are glorious. Through them we know that we are saved by grace through faith, and that faith is the instrument, not the ground, of our salvation. We should thank God for every proposition in his word. Yet at its foundation, our Bible is a story, supported by letters and prophets who explain it. Therefore we must learn to receive it as a story.

God could have given us a systematic theology as his perfect word. He could have given us a bulleted list of every theological truth or a set of flashcards. Instead, he gave us sixty-six books written by more than a dozen authors over a thousand years, telling one great epic of redemption. That is what we are to understand and inhabit. He did not give us an outline of theology because he did not make us as databases for facts. He made us characters in his story, created to live in the world he has made. So we are not to receive this double tragedy in Ezra–Nehemiah as mere historical information—that these events happened in that place at that time. Like all of God’s doctrine, it is meant to be lived, to be incarnated. We are meant to come and live inside their story.

That may sound complicated, but we do it quite naturally. Consider an example from C. S. Lewis’s The Chronicles of Narnia, in Prince Caspian. Peter fights Miraz in single combat rather than letting the armies clash. At one point the much younger Peter believes he is about to lose and be slain. During a break in the fighting, he stands with his brother Edmund. Believing he is about to die, Peter does not weep, cower, or complain. He stands tall, tells Edmund to say goodbye to those he loves, puts on his helmet, and marches back into the fight. It is a remarkable display of courage.

You cannot read that scene to your children at seven o’clock as you prepare to tuck them in, and then at eight o’clock complain to your wife about how difficult your day at work has been. If you do, you have not truly received the story. Stories teach us, but they teach differently than arguments do. Arguments give us answers, questions, and direct commands aimed right at us. Stories capture our imagination with plots to follow, dangers to avoid, and characters to imitate or reject. That is how we must approach the story of Ezra and Nehemiah.

To help us this morning, we will consider three prominent themes in the book. These themes will show us how similar their story is to ours, and how similar their place in God’s story is to ours—though with a glorious twist because of where we stand in the epic of redemption.

Three Prominent Themes for the Series

Theme 1: God’s Steadfast Love and Absolute Power

First, this story displays God’s steadfast love and absolute power. The entire narrative of Ezra–Nehemiah exists only because of God’s steadfast, covenant-keeping love. In chapter 1, verse 1, God stirs the spirit of Cyrus, king of Persia, in order to fulfill the word he had spoken through the prophet Jeremiah. The story begins because God is faithful to his word, steadfast in his love, even though his people are in exile because of their own faithlessness.

The steadfast love of God is their song when they lay the foundation of the temple. It is on display when God does not abandon them at the end of Ezra after their intermarriages; instead, he continues to send blessing. The steadfast love of God keeps the story moving forward. Brothers and sisters, we serve the same God, for he is the same yesterday, today, and forever. We serve the God who rejoices when prodigal sons come home and who welcomes back his wayward bride. We serve the God who, though we are faithless, remains faithful, so that with him there is full forgiveness of sins.

He is not holding your sins over your head this morning. He has removed them as far as the east is from the west because of the sacrifice of Jesus. He is not placing an asterisk beside your good works because of mixed motives. No—our God knows we are far worse than we even realize, and yet what did he do at ten-fifteen this morning but call us into his presence to worship? He has spoken his word to us and will soon invite us to his table in peace, because he is the God of steadfast love. He is more committed to us than we are to him. Though we wander, he remains. Though we waver, he is steadfast. When we doubt, he is sure. When we sin, he forgives. In his free grace he has set his favor on you, and nothing can separate you from the love of God in Christ Jesus.

In Ezra and Nehemiah, this steadfast love is not an abstract principle floating in the air. It is concrete. It appears as political support, godly leaders, timber, and gold. The book repeatedly notes, “The hand of the Lord was on them for good.” Church, the hand of the Lord is on you for good. The steadfast love of God is still tangible. This is why, as we consecrate ourselves to him Sunday after Sunday, we wake up on Monday to see business deals close, to find patience after a long day of a child’s disobedience, to have just the right words for a hard question, to discover mental strength in pain, provision for the bills, love in a difficult marriage, a roof over our heads, and food on our table. If God’s steadfast love were any thicker, you would have to swim through it.

This God is not only the God of steadfast love; he is the God of absolute power. Ezra and Nehemiah show us that he is King over all the earth. In chapter 1, verse 1, he stirs the heart of Cyrus, who imagines himself king of the world. God helps himself to the king’s revenue, his forests, and his citizens. When he does not overthrow a kingdom as in Daniel, he simply turns the king’s heart to do his will. He is the true King of the world, and he still is.

It may feel as though we live in volatile times where no one is in control. It may seem that for the next four years boys will remain boys and girls will remain girls—praise God—but who knows what will follow? It can feel like we are on a shaky carnival ride, hoping the whole thing has been assembled correctly. But that is not the real story. The real story is that we know the God of the ages, who planned not only 2025 but everything that will happen until 3025. He holds the whole world in his hands.

Psalm 2 is still true. The kings of the earth may set themselves against the Lord and his Anointed, seeking to break their bonds. Yet God laughs—with great, mocking belly laughs—and when he catches his breath he declares that he has given all their kingdoms to his beloved Son, with full authority to dash them in pieces like a potter’s vessel. When Ezra–Nehemiah says, “God stirred up the heart of Cyrus king of Persia,” it invites us to live in the true story of the world, where the Most High rules the kingdom of men and does as he pleases. So let us stand in awe of this God this morning. Let us be amazed, humbled, and filled with fear. What kind of God calls his treacherous people back, supplying everything needed to rebuild his capital? What God is the true King over every earthly king? “The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness.” He is our God.

Theme 2: The Kingdom’s Need for the New Covenant

The second theme is that this repeating tragedy reveals that God’s kingdom needs the new covenant. The first theme—God’s character—is true everywhere in the Bible because he is the same yesterday, today, and forever. This second theme is true throughout the Old Testament, but it is especially clear in Ezra and Nehemiah because we stand at the final chapter before Jesus arrives. We watch God’s kingdom restored, and then we watch it crumble.

God supplied every external need, did he not? Political support from the kings of the earth, money, timber, and godly leaders who looked refreshing after the long line of faithless kings. He gave them everything outwardly required, yet the reformation still failed. What does that teach us? The problem was not external. Ezra and Nehemiah show us that God’s people were not merely sick and needing healing, nor weak and needing help. They were dead and needed to be reborn—a valley of dry bones that required the breath of God to blow over them and bring them to life.

This is our story too, is it not? We were dead in our trespasses and sins, following the course of this world and the prince of the power of the air. But God, being rich in mercy, because of the great love with which he loved us, called us out of exile. While we were still sinners, Christ died for us. He has transferred us from the domain of darkness into the kingdom of his beloved Son. The old covenant could not remake them, but God has done what the law, weakened by the flesh, could not do. The new creation has come. In fact, it is here in this room, sitting in your seat. The King has said he is making all things new, and he has begun with us.

See how similar our position is at the beginning of our chapter, and how much hope we should have because of where we stand in God’s story. In Ezra and Nehemiah, God called his people out of exile in Persia to rebuild the temple and the city. He brought them out of Babylon, but he still needed to take Babylon out of them. In our day, he is again building a temple and a city. First Peter chapter 2 tells us that God is building a spiritual house, and we are the living stones. Revelation 21 shows the New Jerusalem, the city of God, coming down from heaven as a bride for the Lamb. Its foundation is the apostles—the church. We are the new temple and the new city God is building.

But this time he has given us the new covenant. He has caused us to be born again. He has fixed what made their reformation fail, so this time the story does not have to end in tragedy. In fact, he has promised it will not. This time it will not end with compromising marriages between the seed of God and the seed of the serpent. It will end at the marriage supper of the Lamb, when all things are made new, every enemy is a footstool, and death itself is swallowed up in victory.

Many Christians speak and act as though we are still living in a chapter of exile or wilderness wandering, as though we remain in Babylon under God’s curse. That is a misreading of our place in the story. We are not in exile. Jesus has led us out of exile through the waters of baptism and brought us into the land to build and to fight. He has brought us out to establish his new temple and his new city. That is why Paul labors so that we would build one another up in love—so that God’s temple and city would grow. In our chapter of the story, we are called to build and fight amid opposition, under God’s protection, in a story that will not end in tragedy.

Before we come to the final theme, let me offer one clarification. In showing you how similar our place in the story is to that of Ezra and Nehemiah, I am not encouraging you to allegorize Scripture. An allegory, like Pilgrim’s Progress, makes every character and event represent an abstract idea. God’s word did not come to us as an allegory, so we must not read it that way. We are not asking what the temple vessels symbolize or claiming that our four pastors represent the leaders in Ezra and Nehemiah. That is not the point. What we see is a pattern repeated throughout Scripture. In Ezra and Nehemiah, God calls his people out of exile to rebuild the temple and the city. In the New Testament we see the same pattern: God building his temple (1 Peter 2) and his city (Revelation 21) after calling us out of exile. We know we will face opposition and persecution. We know God has promised never to leave us or forsake us. We are not allegorizing; we are learning to live inside the story of Scripture and to understand the times in which we live.

Theme 3: A Warning for Uncompromising Loyalty

The final theme is this: even though the broad chapter of God’s story does not end in tragedy, there is no guarantee that our individual stories will not. God always demands faithfulness from his covenant people. Therefore this story warns us that we must remain loyal, because compromise kills reformation. If we become a compromising, mixed, faithless people, God will discipline us and raise up others for his work. We must be a pure and uncompromising people. In Ezra and Nehemiah, the world does not work through them like leaven in dough; rather, they are infected by the world like mold spreading through bread.

Our God will always receive us back. Jesus has already met the demand for perfect obedience. He will always welcome us home, but we must keep returning. And when we return, we cannot come back carrying idols in our pockets. We must return to him with all our heart and all our soul. We have said we long to see the whole Stillwater area bow the knee to Jesus. That must begin with us. We must be unceasingly loyal in every part of life.

You may have heard the idea of the antithesis on Canon Plus. The antithesis is the great divide between the sons of God—with their beliefs and culture—and the seed of the serpent with theirs. Light has no fellowship with darkness. They mix no more than oil and water. In our day, the most dangerous compromise is with the secular lie. That lie tells us that deep down we are all the same. We all want the same things: a nice house, a two-car garage, two and a half children, and a “Hakuna Matata” life with no worries. That is not what God is calling his people to pursue.

The secular lie says that if we simply set aside our religious differences and political convictions, we will discover that we are all basically good and on the same team. It is a lie. There are only two teams in the world: the sons of God and the sons of the serpent, and they have no fellowship with each other. Our religious differences are not peripheral; they are the most fundamental thing about us. If we are to be a pure people, we must ask God to use his word to burn away every trace of compromise left on us by this culture—materialism, secularism, feminism, Marxism. It must all go, especially in our parenting. If we want to raise a generation that will carry the battle forward long after we are cheering them on from the stands of heaven, we must show them there is a real war. Light has no fellowship with darkness. We must give them the example of uncompromising parents.

The secular lie is effective because it first disarms you. Once you believe we are all on the same team, you lay down your weapons. You cease to threaten the enemy and become a distraction to your brothers and sisters. So this morning we must choose this day whom we will serve. You can serve your comfort, believe the secular lie, remain comfortable, and lose your soul. Or you can pick up a shovel and a sword, come and build and fight, and help construct God’s temple and city. Choose this day whom you will serve, and then let it be seen. Let your loyalty show in your parenting and discipline, in your work ethic, in the music you listen to, the movies you watch, the books you read, your household budget, and the love in your marriage. Let every part of life be invaded by our glorious God until no compromise remains. That is what we are aiming for.

Conclusion and Closing Prayer

Let me conclude by summarizing. As I preach through Ezra and Nehemiah over the next twelve to fourteen months, I want us to learn to live inside this story. We have been called out of exile to sin and death, just as they were called out of Persia. We have been called to establish the worship of God as his living temple, just as they were called to rebuild the physical temple. We have been called to build the city of God, just as they rebuilt Jerusalem. They faced enemies, and we should expect the same. They were protected by their God, and so will we be. But God has made us new. He has taken out our heart of stone and given us a heart of flesh, so that though we cannot live perfectly, we can live faithfully. We can keep repenting and believing. We can keep returning and trusting.

So love and trust the God of steadfast love and absolute power. Give him praise for the new covenant. And keep yourself unstained from the world—living in it, but not of it. Amen.

Let me pray for us. Heavenly Father, we praise you and thank you for who you are. We thank you for setting your steadfast love upon us. We thank you for your wholehearted commitment to us, and we ask you to make that truth burn more brightly in our hearts. Make us a people who reflect your unwavering love so that we may be your faithful people. We ask this in the great name of Jesus. Amen.
