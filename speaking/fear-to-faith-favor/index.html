Fear to Faith, Faith to Favor

Ezra 6

Preached by Nathan Colestock — May 11, 2025

Christ the King Church · Stillwater, MN

May 11, 2025Christ the King Church5400 words, 45 min

FROM FAITH TO FAVORTHE WONDER OF UNMERITED REWARD

The main point of my sermon today is this: Although we never deserve God’s favor, God does reward our faithfulness. So stay faithful, and wait on the Lord.

This point comes from the final episode of the story we began in Ezra 3–5. To explain it well, I will do four things.

1. Review where we have been in this series on Ezra–Nehemiah so far.

2. Complete the story we started in Ezra 3–5. We will see the faithfulness of God’s people turn into the favor of God. The story of redemption will come to a joyous end: fear to faith, faith to favor.

3. Ezra 6:14 will take us on a brief excursion into biblical chronology, where we will re-establish our confidence that God’s Word is trustworthy in every detail. Faithful in little, faithful in much.

4. We will close by appreciating the unmerited favor symbolized and celebrated in the Passover, before we ourselves come to the Lord’s Table. Unmerited reward.

REVIEW

Ezra–Nehemiah is part of God’s repeating pattern in which he redeems his people and his people rebel against him. On the whole, the story told in these books teaches us that God’s kingdom needed a new covenant. We will watch God give his people everything they need—godly leaders, political support, monetary and material support, everything. Yet they still turn from him over and over. Their problem is not external. They are not lacking in resources or education. What they need is new hearts. And because they do not have them, their chapter ends in tragedy. It leaves the reader desperate for the Messiah to come and set his people free from sin and death.

As we preach through the story of Ezra–Nehemiah, we are learning to live inside that story, recognizing what has changed for us who stand on this side of the cross. Although their covenant could not bear the weight of lasting spiritual reformation (it was never intended to), the new covenant made in Jesus’ blood can. Now is the age in which the Spirit has been poured out on the world and God’s new city is being built. As we watch Israel build and fight amid fierce opposition, we will build and fight with enemies close at hand. As God was on their side, he is on our side. But because he has circumcised our hearts, we no longer need to go back to our sinful, fleshly ways. We can remain holy and faithful to our God. Jesus has led us out of slavery in Egypt, where sin was our master, and has brought us across the Red Sea. He has led us out of the wilderness of God’s wrath. Our chapter of the story is Joshua taking the land and Nehemiah building and fighting. But the great difference is that now Jesus has died, risen again, and ascended to his throne on high. He has thrown down every ruler and authority and is making all things new. This time our building and fighting does not have to end in tragedy, because in God’s great faithfulness he will hold us fast.

So far in our series we have preached through Ezra 1–5. In chapters 1–2 we saw God send his people back to the land with the mission to rebuild the temple and the city. Their mission was about restoring the right worship of God by rebuilding the whole society that supports that worship. That is why they did not simply send back a few priests to start sacrifices. They sent back a whole population because they planned to remake a civilization.

Toward this grand mission, God assigns them all different roles. Some are priests and some are farmers. Some are blacksmiths and some are shepherds. Reformation is not the work of a few elite believers. It is the work of the masses who reverence and serve God in all of life—who embrace their calling so fully that they believe they are in full-time ministry wherever God has placed them. Since the mission God has sent us on encompasses all of life, there are no part-timers. All our work is sacred unto God.

The call of gospel ministry, which we all share, is to capture and cultivate every area of life so that all of creation joins in the chorus of praise. Like Israel, our mission encompasses all of life. Each of us is given a unique calling—lawyer, business owner, homemaker, teacher, salesman, delivery driver, financial analyst, toddler, student. All of us have been called by God to serve at our station. And he has given us a common prayer to guide us: “on earth as it is in heaven.” Lord, may your will be done on earth as it is in heaven. Wherever we are, that is what we are praying, seeking, and serving God for.

By his grace, we will work like leaven through a loaf here in Stillwater so that God’s glory will cover this corner of earth like the waters cover the sea. That is what I mean when I say we are about building the city of God. We are about being and building a culture here that glorifies God all the way down to how we eat and drink and dress, and all the way up to the children we enculturate, the businesses we manage, and the policies we advocate for. This is “Big God” theology pressed into all of our little lives. It is the Christian Hedonist’s heart applied to every moment.

Ezra 3–5 gave us our first lesson in what building that city will be like. It warns us to seek first the kingdom of God, lest the fear of man or worldly desire choke our faith. And it encourages us to confess our sin and repent when we fear man rather than God.

You will remember that the returnees start off well. Although they are afraid of the people of the land, they put their trust in God and gather to build the altar. Their first priority is worship. As should ours be.

But Israel’s faith soon turned to fear. When the political pressure really mounted, they caved in. They compromised on God. They could not stay separate from the world and holy to God because at heart they were still part of the world; they were still in the flesh.

This compromise comes in chapter 4 when they stop building the temple for fear of their political opponents. The lawfare of their enemies succeeds. They become convinced that their own safety and comfort are more important than rebuilding God’s kingdom. The ruined temple lying in their midst like a dead body is an apt picture of their spiritual condition.

God was so gracious that he sent prophets to strengthen the people in chapter 5. Their fear turns back to faith again. They repent of fearing man rather than God. They start building again out of faith. But no sooner do they begin than their enemies apply political pressure once more. Look with me at the text.

FAITH TO FAVOR

In Ezra 5:3–6, Tattenai and his associates get involved and write to Darius. They ask the king to search the royal archives (5:17) to see whether Cyrus really decreed that the temple should be rebuilt. The king’s letter from chapter 4 said that he did not want the city rebuilt until he gave permission, but it said nothing about the temple.

Much to the dismay of God’s enemies, Darius writes back to inform them that not only did he find Cyrus’s decree, but that decree also provides the dimensions and the funding for the building of the temple. So King Darius commands Tattenai to leave the Jews alone (6:7). You can tell that he knows Tattenai would like to deal severely with God’s people. He also commands Tattenai to give them anything they need from the tribute that he collects (6:8–9).

To appreciate how big a deal this is, you need to remember how precarious Israel’s situation in the land was. They were an ethnic and religious minority who had traveled across the world with only what they could carry. They were trying to rebuild their capital city, restart an economy, produce the tools they needed to rebuild the temple, clean up the rubble—all while surrounded by enemies who had been threatening them from the moment they arrived.

And right when they start obeying God again, the very worst thing that could happen happens. The leader of their enemies takes a personal interest in their project. A powerful, well-connected, established political leader against a ragtag band of returnees who had just arrived? This was not going to end well. Their lives and their families’ lives were at stake. Maybe it felt unfair to them: “God, we just started obeying! Why didn’t you save us from this?” But they do not complain or grumble. When they started building again, they knew that faithfulness would put a target on their backs. Instead, they simply keep on faithfully obeying in what they can control and trusting God with what they cannot. This is faith: loyal reliance. While they wait for a letter back from the king—though they know he might command every stone they have placed to be torn down—they work heartily with the time God gives them. And God rewards their faithful labor.

When the king’s letter comes, it is everything they could have hoped for and more. Look at it with me. It literally says in 6:7, “Let the work of the house of God alone.” In a great twist of irony, Tattenai will now be making deliveries to the house of God that he wanted to hinder, providing supplies for the sacrifices. And just when you think it cannot get any better, it does. Look at verses 11–12. Darius knows that someone like Tattenai would love to tamper with his command so that he can persecute the Jews. But Darius writes, “Also, I make a decree that if anyone alters this edict, a beam shall be pulled out of his house and he shall be impaled on it, and his house shall be made a dunghill.”

In other words, “If you mess with the work of the house of God, I will kill you on a beam from your own house and bury you in the rubble.” Then in verse 12 he even calls on God to curse anyone who would attack his house.

This letter provides the Jews with the security they need to finish the house. And so they do not waste any time. Just four years after they had begun working again, the temple was finished. In 6:16 they dedicate the temple. In verse 17 they make the first sacrifices. In verse 18 they set the Levites in place over the worship. It is a monumental moment. The central task is complete. God’s people can worship God on his holy hill again. This is the linchpin for rebuilding the city and society. As many have said, there is no culture without a cultus (worship). And now their culture has what it needs to flourish.

Let us step back for a moment to reflect on the movement of this story. This is a story of redemption. It is God’s favorite kind of story: fear to faith to favor. He takes sinful, fearful people, strengthens them with simple faith, and pours out his favor on those he has made faithful. He turns their greatest fear into great blessing. He makes their terror their deliverance.

God has done this many times before. Think of Jacob, whose greatest fear was losing his son Benjamin. But when he sent him with his brothers back to Egypt, not only was Benjamin saved, but the whole family was saved and Jacob received back his son Joseph. Think of Abraham, called to sacrifice his own son Isaac. His willingness to do so prompted God’s blessing. Think of the story of Esther, when the plot of Haman turns out for the deliverance and prosperity of the Jews. Think of Jesus himself, who hung cursed on a tree and lay cold in a tomb before he turned the grave into a gate to everlasting glory.

God has a knack for turning desperate circumstances into delightful surprises when we simply stay faithful—faithful in what we can control, and trusting God with what we cannot. I think of Cowper’s famous hymn:

“Ye fearful saints, fresh courage take, the clouds ye so much dread are big with mercy, and shall break with blessings on your head.”

This is why we can take hope even in the moments of blackest despair. God loves turning mourning into dancing. “When I am afraid, I put my hope in you.” Your God has very literally promised to wipe away every tear. In the deepest pain and darkest trials, this is what you need to know: Jesus loves you.

This morning, you might feel like you are stuck between Ezra 5 and Ezra 6. You have been trying to be faithful. You have tried to stick to the straight and narrow even though the clouds gathered and your fears came rolling in. Your children will not honor you, you are still single and long to be married, family and business relationships just get more and more strained, your future hopes seem dashed. Hear this: your hope is in God. He turned a sepulcher into your salvation. While you wait for the clouds to break into blessings, you can trust him, cling to him, wait on him. Jesus loves you.

Cast your burdens onto Jesus. Trust that he cares for you. Be faithful with what he has given you to do. And trust him for everything you cannot control. Then wait for him to finish your story. God does not like tragedies. He writes stories of redemption. “Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God.”

FAITHFUL IN LITTLE

Now we need to step back for a moment to study a problem this text raises for us. Look for a moment at 6:14. It says that Cyrus, Artaxerxes, and Darius helped to build the temple. You probably skipped right over this because we have trained ourselves to think that details like this do not matter. But they do. Who was the one that stopped the rebuilding of the temple in chapter 4? It was Artaxerxes. It says in verses 23–24 that after that letter the work on the temple stopped until the second year of Darius. But now in chapter 6 it says that Artaxerxes was part of helping construct the temple. What is going on?

I am going to take you down a rabbit hole of biblical chronology for a few minutes. But before I do, I want to tell you why we are going down there. This is the point: we should trust God’s Word.

God’s Word gives us the standard for trustworthiness: “If you are faithful in little you will be faithful in much.” We cannot simply write off little things in the Bible as unimportant and say that we trust it where it is important. It is because the Bible is trustworthy in the little things—in the names and dates and places—that we can trust it in the big things, like the resurrection and our future hope. That is the main point of going down this rabbit hole. As we do, it will explain some of the issues we have already seen and more that will keep popping up in Ezra–Nehemiah.

Printed in your worship guide notes is what scholars believe is the best guess on the names and dates of the first five Persian kings. Everyone agrees that this story happens in this time period. The settled view of most interpreters is that the kings mentioned in Scripture are the same ones mentioned by the ancient historian Ptolemy. So Cyrus is Cyrus, Darius is Darius I, and Artaxerxes is Artaxerxes I (or Artaxerxes Longimanus). But you might already see the problem. Artaxerxes, the king in Ezra 4, comes chronologically after Darius, who is mentioned in chapter 5.

The way most interpreters deal with this is to say that Ezra 4 is actually chronologically before Ezra 5, but it is placed there because it fits thematically. Now, I do not want to sound as though that is a ridiculous idea, because people a lot smarter than I am believe it. It could very well be that I am wrong here. But frankly, that sounds like special pleading. There is nothing in chapter 4 to suggest that it is out of order. In fact, verses 23–24 seem to demand that it is chronologically before chapter 5.

That leads me to the conclusion that the Bible is not using the same names for the kings as Ptolemy. Now that is not a problem. In fact, Ezra–Nehemiah was written long before any historian ever put together a list of the kings’ names and dates. They knew far better who the kings were and what they were called. More importantly, they would know whether the kings went by just one name or not.

For us, it is customary to call each king by just one name. But that is not necessarily the case in every culture. In fact, we know that these names were throne names. They were used by more than one king. After they have been anglicized they simply sound like personal names. But in their original language they have clear meanings:

  • Darius – doer of good
  • Xerxes – hero among kings
  • Artaxerxes – king of justice

Now, following a few others (Doug Wilson, James Jordan, Philip Mauro, Martin Anstey), I think that these kings used more than one throne name at a time. And this is what explains how Artaxerxes could be mentioned as helping to build the temple in Ezra 6:14. I think that Artaxerxes and Darius are the same king. I think you could translate Ezra 6:14 something like this: “by decree of Cyrus and Darius, that is, Artaxerxes…”

Now, if that is the case, the timeline of the story just got a lot shorter. The story ends in the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes. If that is Artaxerxes Longimanus, the story takes about one hundred years from Cyrus’s decree to the end. But if that is the thirty-second year of King Darius, who was also called Artaxerxes, there are only about fifty years.

That difference allows us to test this theory that perhaps the whole story happens in a shorter amount of time under just one king, Darius.

The first way we could try to prove this theory wrong is by showing that the years of the kings’ reigns contradict one another. If it says the fifteenth year of Artaxerxes and then after that it says the third year of Darius, you would know that they are different kings. But that never happens. In fact, the dates line up to make it look like it is the same king. Notice in 6:15 the temple is finished in the sixth year of Darius. Then in 7:7 it is the seventh year of Artaxerxes. It lines right up. So we cannot prove my theory that it is the same king wrong that way.

Another way we could prove it wrong is by using the lists of names provided in these books. If the story was short—say, fifty years—we should find plenty of people who came with Zerubbabel at the beginning still alive with Nehemiah when they make a covenant. But if that was more like one hundred years later, there should not really be anyone still alive who came with Zerubbabel.

Fortunately, Nehemiah records both of these lists for us. You can find a list of the leaders who came with Zerubbabel in 7:7 (feel free to ransack this for baby names). Chapter 12 gives a list of priests and Levites in the days of Zerubbabel as well. Then in chapter 10 Nehemiah lists those who sign the covenant with him.

If there were around one hundred years between Zerubbabel and the dedication of the wall, no one should be on both lists. Right? (Unlike in the American Social Security system, people do not usually live deep into their hundreds.)

But lots of people are listed as being alive in the days of both Zerubbabel and Nehemiah. I provided a table in your worship guide so that you can see it for yourself.

In my eyes, these names are pretty good evidence that this story did not take one hundred years. And therefore the end of the story was not under Artaxerxes Longimanus. Instead, it took place under Darius (who was also called Artaxerxes). In fact, I think that the Bible itself pushes us toward this conclusion.

Do you remember that Daniel (chapter 9) received word that after the seventy years of exile were done for God’s people, God had decreed seventy weeks of years? Those seventy weeks were split into seven weeks and then sixty-two weeks. There would be seven weeks of years before Jerusalem was rebuilt, and then sixty-two weeks before the Anointed One would be cut off. Well, seven weeks of years is forty-nine years, which fits this shorter timeline perfectly. Forty-nine years takes you from Cyrus’s decree right to the end of the reign of Darius.

So, until proven otherwise, I am going to interpret and tell the story that way, with a shorter chronology than most interpreters. I think that Artaxerxes and Darius are throne names for the same individual and that the story of Ezra–Nehemiah takes place in about fifty years because of the evidence in the lists of names and the internal consistencies about the names of kings (like Artaxerxes helping with the temple). It is not a new view. John Gill took this view in the eighteenth century, and when he did he was going along with others.

But there are two reasons that I think I might be wrong. The first reason I might be wrong is that it seems like Ezra–Nehemiah introduces these kings as different people sometimes. It is not one hundred percent clear, but it seems like it. For instance, in chapter 7 it says, “Now after this, in the reign of Artaxerxes king of Persia…” Normally, I would say that sounds like a transition to a new king. So this might be evidence that I am wrong. But since that would create other problems I cannot explain, right now I think that Ezra and Nehemiah are purposefully using these throne names because of what they mean. Darius does good to them. Then they switch to Artaxerxes who will do justice for Ezra. But like I said, I might be wrong.

Another reason I might be wrong is this. If Daniel’s weeks are literal years, then we should be able to check our math by subtracting the year Cyrus made his decree, 536 BC, from the seventy weeks of years (490) and get the year the Anointed One was cut off (AD 30). But instead, we have an extra seventy or eighty years. In other words, the whole BC dating system is about seventy years too long if I am right. Some biblical chronologers are convinced that there are extra years among the Persian kings, whom we have thought were more than one king when they were really using many throne names. But this is outside my area of expertise, and a big yellow flag saying it might be wrong. Martin Anstey and his popularizer Philip Mauro are the men to check if you want to learn more about this.

So where does this leave us? It should bring us back to the point we started with. We need to trust the Bible. For centuries scholars have claimed the Bible was inaccurate in this or that date or location. And over and over it has been vindicated. God has proven that his Word is accurate, even in the little things. So we need to build our lives and our interpretations on what Scripture says. We should feel no pressure to bend Scripture to fit what archaeology or historians are saying right now. They will say something different twenty years from now. Instead, we can trust that the Bible is sufficient to interpret its own story. And as we study an issue like the chronology of Ezra, we must do so without compromising our trust in God’s Word. Do not say, “I guess it got this little thing wrong. That is okay.” You can trust the Bible in the big things because it is right in the little things.

PASSOVER FAVOR

Now we get to come back to Ezra 6:19 to consider God’s favor in the Passover. There is a big shift that is invisible to us in our Bibles at 6:19. Starting in 4:7, the beginning of the letters and the compromise of God’s people, the story was written in Aramaic (the language of the land). I think that Ezra shifted to Aramaic because this story of fear to faith to favor was open for the world to look into. It did not make the Jews look like mighty or spiritually mature people. But it does make God look like a faithful and mighty God.

We know from Daniel that Persia has an evil spiritual power ruling over it, the “Prince of Persia” whom Gabriel battles in Daniel 10 to bring the prophecy of the seventy weeks. But this story being open for all the world to read demonstrates that God is the one who is really in charge of the kingdom of Persia. He turns their kings (who were called sons of the gods) to do whatever he wills. When he wants to, Yahweh turns the hearts of the sons of wicked gods for the benefit of his son Israel.

So this story of God’s incredible power and redeeming love is on full display for the nations, written in their common tongue. Then in 6:19 the story switches back to Hebrew as they celebrate their most identity-forming feast: Passover.

Passover is fitting for them to celebrate after God’s incredible loving favor in giving them the support they need to complete the temple. God turned their fear of political retribution into their salvation. Now they are celebrating Passover, when God turned the story of Joseph—a young man sold into slavery by his own brothers—into salvation for his family. And then he turned their four hundred years of unjust slavery into the fulfillment of his ancient promise to Abraham to make his seed a great nation. This feast is about how God redeems awful circumstances and turns them to blessing.

And the whole feast revolves around the symbol of the Passover lamb: substitutionary atonement, merciful favor, unmerited kindness. The innocence of another provided to save the guilty, with faith to hide in God’s command. The faith that God rewards is not proud; it is counting on grace because it knows that it falls short. So in 6:20 they slaughter the Passover lamb.

Verse 21 makes clear what was always true in Israel. Anyone who worshiped Yahweh was welcome to join in the feast. Far from being isolationist, God’s people invite outsiders into their most sacred religious and cultural feast so long as they worship Yahweh. The only requirement to come and celebrate the salvation of God is humble faith.

See in the story from fear to faith to favor and in the symbol of this feast how these two truths hold gloriously together:

1. God rewards the faithful.

2. The reward is never deserved.

God’s people have compromised on him, but he graciously brings them back to faith through the prophets. Obviously they do not deserve God’s favor. They just walked away from him. But when they turn back to him and obey his commands, God rewards their simple faith by enabling their building.

God did the same with the Passover. Israel grumbled against God and Moses as God saved them from their Egyptian masters, defeating their gods. But still God provided them with the Passover lamb to cover their households from the angel of death. And those who believed God applied the blood of the lamb to their doorposts to be saved by simple faith.

God’s favor is never deserved. These stories make that crystal clear. We can never think, “I have been faithful, God…where is my reward? Serve it up!”

That kind of thinking is at the heart of that pernicious error: the prosperity gospel. It confuses God’s gracious rewards with a kind of divine paycheck.

Prosperity-gospel teachers preach a strict cause-and-effect relationship between good works (especially “sowing” money in their ministry) and receiving in-kind rewards from God (like “reaping” money after giving). Really it is gross manipulation.

A more subtle kind of prosperity gospel preaches that when you come to Jesus you will get everything you want. Which is true if Jesus changes your heart. But this kind of prosperity gospel really just aims to fill church pews. It insinuates that if you come to church your marriage will improve, your kids will be obedient, your work will take off. It really views church as a kind of spiritual therapy that will help you get what you want.

Both the more blatant and the more subtle forms of prosperity thinking compromise in their loyalty to God. They treat him like a vending machine. They are really just karma dressed up in thinly Christian clothing, benefiting some teachers and those close to them.

God does favor the faithful. But the faithful are not trying to manipulate God.

This manipulating attitude is tragic. It is tragic because it is missing out. Like those who followed Jesus just for a free lunch. They walked in the footsteps of the bread of life, too excited by the bread he could multiply to receive life for their souls. Tragic.

If you sometimes think, “Why did that bad thing happen, God? Why am I not happy? I am reading my Bible and praying every day. I am giving to the church. Why won’t you give me what I want?” you are thinking right in line with the prosperity gospel, trying to manipulate God with your good works into giving you what you want. If that is where you are this morning, I can tell you two things for sure:

1. Whatever you are trying to convince God to give you has become an idol for you. It is eclipsing God in your life and you need to put it back in its proper place (whether that means removing it entirely or simply reordering your desires).

2. Simple faith is the cure. If you are trying to manipulate or trick God into giving you what you want, you have forgotten the first lesson about God. He is a good Father. Jesus loves you. You need to let go of whatever it is you want so badly, and hold on to the great truth that your God is good and he loves you. Trust him to give or not to give.

CONCLUSION

So from this sermon I want you to take away this: God rewards his faithful ones. But there is no manipulating God. He rewards simple faith. In fact, he loves to pour out incredible blessings on his faithful people. He frequently turns their terrors and trials into the key ingredient for their salvation. He turns fears to favor through faith.

If you are stuck in trial, waiting in the storm for the clouds to break into mercy, trust God. Hold fast to your first lesson: Jesus loves you.

And as you hold fast to that first lesson, do not forget where it comes from: “Jesus loves me, this I know, for the Bible tells me so.” Trust God’s Word in the big things and the little things. It is trustworthy (regardless of what you think about the chronology of Ezra–Nehemiah).

As we come now to the Passover Feast made new—where our Passover Lamb gave himself for us to turn our sin into forgiveness and our separation into adoption—we come as those simply soaked in the favor of God. So let us keep the feast with joy, like Israel did. God has washed away our sins, given us each other, filled us with love for one another, and provided a place to worship. At this table, all things are being made new through simple faith.

Let us pray.
