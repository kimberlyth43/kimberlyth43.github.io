How to Avoid Sabotage

Nehemiah 6

Preached by Nathan Colestock — May 24, 2026

Christ the King Church · Stillwater, MN

How to Avoid Sabotage (Nehemiah 6) #sermon *May 24, 2026 | Christ the King Church in Stillwater, MN * **Scripture ** Nehemiah 6 **Main Point ** Nehemiah’s example teaches us how to avoid sabotage by being sober-minded. Introduction In Matthew 10:16 our Lord warned his disciples, “Behold, I am sending you out as sheep in the midst of wolves, so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” In this passage, Nehemiah is an example of how to be wise as servants. Nehemiah has been a good example for us through this whole story. So far we have seen him continually stand strong in the face of opposition. With God's help, he has been a godly example of self-sacrificing to accomplish the mission that God sent him on. In this story overall, Nehemiah has left his job as cupbearer to come to Jerusalem and rebuild the walls. He organized and galvanized the people of Israel to devote themselves to building the wall and to stand firm when Sanballat and Tobiah organized a military attack against them. He was under Nehemiah's leadership that they strapped their swords to their sides and they kept the work going despite the opposition. In the Nehemiah 5 sermon, I talked about the fear of God being a sword against our sin, a shield against compromise, and fuel for sacrifice. In this passage, the fear of God remains Nehemiah's shield against compromise. Another way to say this is that the Fear of God puts steel in our spine so that we can stand firm against our enemies rather than be cowed by them or tricked by them. Summarize the text and its main point In Nehemiah 6, our text for this morning, the enemies of God are looking for another way to stop the building, which verse one says is going well. They already tried ambushing the workers. That's why they're working on the wall with swords strapped to their sides. The opportunity for a surprise attack is gone. So now they need to find a new strategy; something a little more subtle. In Nehemiah 6 they try three different attacks.

Dishonest diplomacy. They invite Nehemiah to come out and meet them. But Nehemiah sees through their plot and refuses to join them, even though they tried over and over again. 2. **Threats and slander **. They say, "It's reported that you intend to rebel...and that you wish to become king" (6:6). They try to convince Nehemiah to come to the table and negotiate so that they won't write another letter to the king. But Nehemiah doesn't take the bait. He says, "You are inventing them out of your own mind" (6:8). 3. Attempt to discredit. Now they're getting desperate, so they bribe some of the prophets of God to try to scare Nehemiah. Since they can't convince Nehemiah to step into their trap by pressuring him out to them, they turn up the pressure at home. They attempt to defeat him by discrediting him. If they can get him to run away and hide in the temple then he will lose the support of the people. But Nehemiah sees right through this as well. He would rather die than be a coward. The main thing happening in this text is that Nehemiah is attacked and responds shrewdly—wise as serpents. We are supposed to learn from Nehemiah's godly example. Nehemiah’s shrewd response is not to negotiate with terrorists. He has the spine to stand firm because he fears God rather than man. All of these are attempts to sabotage Nehemiah's good efforts at building the wall by exploiting Nehemiah's good character. These are the political or the emotional version of terrorist tactics. Terrorists hide themselves in schools and hospitals and behind women and children to exploit the good guy's good character who doesn't want to hurt the innocent. They exploit the goodness of their enemy to their advantage. That's what Nehemiah's enemies are looking to do to him. When they attack him with false diplomacy, they are looking to hijack the good desire to live at peace. When they attack with the threat of a bad reputation, they are attacking the desire to see justice and bring understanding. When they try to trick him by bribing the prophets, they are looking to exploit his trust in God and therefore in God's messengers. In other words, this text is about how to avoid sabotage. And the God-given antidote that Nehemiah embodies is sober-mindedness. *Why you should care * Maybe you’re thinking, “I get why politicians need to avoid sabotage…but what I do care?” Perhaps you have read or heard of Joe Rigney's book *Emotional Sabotage *, which is excellent and you should all read it. The subtitle of his book explains why everybody should care about how to avoid

sabotage, “Resisting the anxiety that will wreck your family, destroy your church, and ruin the world.” In his book, he explains why we especially must learn to be sober-minded. Our society did away with the social order that organized us. We rejected the authority of husbands over wives, parents over children, teachers over students. The obvious problem is that God didn't design us to be our own authority. He designed us to live under His authority. The world was made with hierarchy: Parents and children Husbands and wives Teachers and students Magistrates and citizens Older and younger When this authority is ignored, or, as in the case of our world, intentionally dismantled, fear, disquiet, and anxiety (opposites of sober-mindedness) reign supreme. Without the authorities God put in place doing their God-given job of providing order and structure to each area of societal life, society descends into egalitarian anxiety. Sabotage is so useful because it’s a way to undermine the leadership and authority without a frontal assault. We all face sabotage in almost every sphere of our lives. A manipulating wife is tempted toward dishonest diplomacy to convince her husband to change his mind. Upset children resort to threats and slander when their parents won't give them their way. Miffed co-workers resort to tricks, attempting to discredit our ability or character. Since our society (and our sin nature) hates authority, everyone who has some authority (and that’s most people) needs to understand how to avoid sabotage. I've sometimes talked to people who seem to think that avoiding this kind of sabotage or manipulation is a question of technique or some special secret relationship knowledge. But what this passage shows us is that there is no special technique or knowledge. Avoiding sabotage is as simple as staying sober-minded. If your soul's anchor is firmly caught in the fear of God, the wind and waves of the demands, sabotage, and manipulations from others can blow against you, but they will have no effect. The opposite of sober-mindedness is being drunk, inebriated, intoxicated with the feelings and urgency of the moment. The opposite of sober-mindedness is to be under the influence of other people instead of having your faculties under your own control. I love Rigney’s definition of sober-mindedness: clarity of mind, steadiness of soul, readiness to act.

Being sober-minded means that you see clearly. Your vision isn’t clouded or fuzzy. It means your are firmly rooted. You aren’t blown about by your own feelings or the feelings of others. Where does this sober-mindedness come from? Of course it’s partially the result of experience and maturity. Like a general who’s been in dozens of battles isn’t shaken when the fighting begins, or a first responder coming to his 300th call, or the ER doctor calm under pressure after years of dealing with emergencies, as God kindly gives us more experience we often grow in sober-mindedness. That’s why Titus 2:2 says that especially older men ought to be sober-minded. But ultimately, biblical sober-mindedness needs an anchor deeper than just our experience/confidence in ourselves. The mental clarity and soul-steadiness that sober-mindedness require, needs an unshakable hope and fear of God. Remember the parable Jesus tells. The man who builds his house on the sand loses everything when the rains fall and the storms come. But the man who builds his house on the solid rock, the storm can’t move him. That’s true of faith. If you build your life anywhere but on the rock of Christ, you’ll be washed away in the end when God judges the world. But it’s also true on a smaller scale. If you anchor your soul anywhere but in Christ, you’ll be washed away by the storms of life. When the pressure turns up you’ll panic. And that’s not what God wants for us. He wants us to grow up to mature in Christ. Jesus is the great dragon slayer, and he's calling us to learn to imitate him and so persevere in the face of great and powerful evil. Some of that evil will be the kind of sabotage used against Nehemiah in this passage. *Outline/Thesis * This sermon from Nehemiah 6 is about how to avoid sabotage. We will learn from the example of Nehemiah how to avoid sabotage by fearing God and therefore being sober-minded. We're going to look at each of the three attacks against Nehemiah and his responses. 1. Dishonest diplomacy 2. Threat of slander 3. Attempt to discredit Dishonest Diplomacy. "Come and let us meet together" The Attack We’ll look first at their attempt to trick Nehemiah with dishonest diplomacy in 6:1–4. Sanballot, Tobiah, and Geshem, who are the kings and leaders of their enemies, hear that Nehemiah and the

people built up the wall significantly. The project has gone on astonishingly well despite Sanballot’ scheming. A frontal military assault didn’t work, so now they’re trying to sabotage Nehemiah. “Come, let us meet together at Hekathirum in the plain of Ono,” they say in verse 2. But Nehemiah knows right away they intended to do him harm. In this case, it looks like their plan is to get Nehemiah out to the plain and ambush him there to assassinate him. But pay special attention to how they are trying to get him to come out. They are looking to exploit Nehemiah's desire to live at peace and be a reasonable ruler who engages in diplomacy with his neighbors against him. They are acting like they are reasonable rulers looking to get together with their Israelite counterpart. They're hiding behind a fake desire for peace and cooperation. They're looking to leverage Nehemiah's true desire for peace and cooperation in order to sabotage his project. When it doesn't work with their first communication, they try over and over and over again. The attempt here is to turn up the pressure. Every time Nehemiah says no, he has to remain clear in his own mind that the kings are real enemies and they haven't just had a misunderstanding. He has to be steady enough to keep the support of the people who would love to come to an agreement with these kings so that they wouldn't be attacked. Each ask turns up the pressure and makes it harder to say, “No.” To be crystal clear, the play here is this: they haven't been able to make Nehemiah budge, so now, instead of an obvious attack, they try sticking a "cooperative" crowbar to get leverage over him and pull him away from the city long enough to do him harm. The trick is to weaponize his virtuous desire for peace and cooperation against him. *Nehemiah’s Response * Now, this attack only has the chance to work because Nehemiah is a good man. He doesn't want to blow off Israel's neighbors. He's not trying to be a bad neighbor, but Nehemiah is also sober-minded. He's not driven by the desire to keep up a collaborative appearance or stay in the good graces of enemies who could make his life hard. He knows the difference between his friends and his enemies. He hasn't let time confuse him about what the goal of these men is. He knows that they are dead set against the mission that Nehemiah is on to establish God's people in the land again. Since he's not trying to act like everyone is on his side, Nehemiah can respond shrewdly. He says, "I'm doing a great work and I cannot come down." The three kings send him the same message four other times, and he has the fortitude to say, “no” every time. Notice that Nehemiah's response does not apologize for doing the work. He doesn’t try to soften the

blow that he’s accomplishing something they don't want. He doesn't hide that he's doing the work. He doesn't pretend that the wall isn't going so great. He affirms, "Yes, I'm working on the wall. I think it's very important. In fact, it's so important I'm not going to come down and talk to you about it." He doesn't try to smooth things over or pretend that they don't have any disagreements. He openly acknowledges what the issue is and tells them that he's not changing course. This is crucial for not being sabotaged. You've got to know who your enemies are, and not act like you trust them. You can't act like everybody is on your side, or if they just understood fully, they would be on your side. If Nehemiah had done the "nice thing" and cooperated, he would have found himself bleeding out in the plain of Ono. Instead, he is shrewd enough to understand who his enemies are. He is also strong enough to stand firm even though they ask repeatedly. Nehemiah isn't willing to budge or compromise about whether he's going to build this wall and establish God's people back in their land. He's also sure that Sanballat, Tobiah, and Gashmu don't want that to happen. There are two lessons to learn from Nehemiah’s response: 1. Don't lose track of who your enemies are. Don't forget which people don't want to see your project succeed. Don't let the passage of time make you assume that their disagreement or attacks were really just a big misunderstanding. 2. Don’t fall prey to dishonest diplomacy. When you're working on something that you are unwilling to budge on, it's not worth "compromising" or "cooperating" with those who don't actually want what you want. In Your Life So, how are we supposed to avoid dishonest diplomacy in our own lives? Well, first we need to see it. The call for constant cooperation from those who don't actually want to cooperate is at the very least a distraction from the mission. If you're clear on what you should be doing, don't let people pull you off of it. Notice that Nehemiah's essential response is to ignore their desire for diplomacy. He says, "I'm too busy. I'm doing too much good, important work. I'm not going to stop it to come chat with you." This requires that you be clear enough about what you're trying to do that you actually know what a distraction looks like and that you have the fortitude to say “no.” Attempts at false diplomacy will continue over and over and over again, especially if you take the meeting. The play here is to use your good desire to be reasonable and a team player and cooperative, and not standoffish or a jerk, against you by pulling you away from important work and using meetings to harm or hinder you. So when you feel like you're being forced into talking with people who don't

actually want what you want, you should think twice before you take that meeting. As we apply this to our lives, we don't only have to apply it to the kind of enemies that are looking to kill us. Sometimes our family and friends and closest loved ones line up as our momentary enemies on a particular issue or topic. We can still apply what we are learning from Nehemiah then. The principle Nehemiah is working with is that he refuses to cooperate with those who are trying to manipulate him into putting down his important work. And as he refuses, he remains sober-minded, not nervous or pulled around by their demands or desires. So let me give you some examples where dishonest diplomacy might show up in your life: Perhaps at work you may have a co-worker who doesn't really seem to be working in your best interest. They could use meetings to sneak themselves into the project and become a stake- holder who sabotages the project. Or one of your direct reports might not like one of your recent decisions and ask for meeting after meeting after meeting to talk it over so that they can get on the same page. What they really want is for you to change their mind, and they're looking to pressure you to change your mind by talking about the decision they don't like with you over and over again. Children may also do the same thing to parents when parents have made a decision that they're upset about. They may try to weaponize their parent's desire to be a reasonable and just parent who keeps communication lines open with their children in order to try to pressure their parents to get what they want. And it can happen in extended families. Perhaps you're looking to establish biblical discipline in your household, and extended family members are uncomfortable with that. They ask to talk about it over and over and over so that they can tell you about their concerns. We could continue to multiply examples, but I think you get the point. What does it look like to follow Nehemiah's godly example? 1. Be resolved not to set down your mission because it's making others uncomfortable. The discomfort and agitation of someone else ought not to dissuade you from doing the good work God has prepared you for. 2. Do not give in to dishonest diplomatic demands. Don't take the meeting. 3. Don't pretend like there's no disagreement. Acknowledge your difference and kindly but clearly make sure they know that you're not setting down your mission. 4. Expect repeated requests, and every time respond consistently. What this requires isn't some special technique or secret knowledge, but simply sober-mindedness. Be clear about what your mission is. Be clear about whether the person asking you to cooperate with

them wants to see that mission accomplished or not. Be steady. Don't be steered or swayed by their disappointment when you continue. Keep on with the mission. Threat of Slander. "You wish to become their king" The Attack When Nehemiah won't come to the table for their requests for cooperation and diplomacy, they turn up the pressure on Nehemiah by threatening to slander him. In v. 5 they send a servant with a letter saying, It is reported among the nations, and Gashem also says it, that you and the Jews intend to rebel. That is why you are building the wall. According to these reports, you wish to become their king, and you have also set up prophets to proclaim concerning you in Jerusalem, "There is no king in Judah." Now the king will hear of these reports, so come and let us take counsel together. There are two pieces to this attack: 1. To threaten Nehemiah by informing the king that Nehemiah will rebel. That would, of course, be disastrous for Nehemiah. Nehemiah desires to have a good reputation, especially with the king. This desire for a good reputation is not bad. But these rulers are weaponizing that desire for a good reputation against Nehemiah so that he's tempted to swerve from the mission God has given him in order to preserve his reputation with the king. They are hoping that this threat will bring Nehemiah to the table to negotiate with them. 2. They send this communication as an open letter instead of privately to Nehemiah. This means that it's not just Nehemiah who hears of this threatened slander. It's also all the people in Jerusalem. Their goal, and we can see it in verse 9, is to frighten the people so that they would drop the work and leave off doing it in exchange for the king not hearing that they intend to rebel. It creates a crisis of leadership for Nehemiah so that he needs to keep everyone committed to the project now that there is a new danger in the way of continuing on. They are essentially saying "We’re going to tell the king that you want to be king instead. That won’t make him happy and you’ll be in trouble and the king will come to put a stop to your “uprising.” So, you better come down so that we can sort out this "misunderstanding" together.” Their hope is that by frightening the people and Nehemiah with the threat of the king's military involvement, they will leave off building. Sending it as an open letter also is an attempt to try to take the decision out of Nehemiah's hands. They're hoping that the people will hear and, regardless of what Nehemiah wants, they will leave the building because they are afraid of its consequences. This is

weaponizing Nehemiah's desire for a good reputation and for peace among his men. Nehemiah's response. Nehemiah fights their lies like a true soldier of the King of kings. He fights with truth. He responds, saying, "No such things as you say have been done, for you are inventing them out of your own mind." In other words, “We both know you're making all that up, and I'm not changing course based on your mind.” Again, he is crystal clear with them. I will not put down the good work God has given me for anything. He also doesn't let them off the hook. He doesn't respond, saying, "Really, who did you hear that from?" He doesn't try to track down the beginning of the rumors. He's not interested in debating their truthfulness. He tells them outright, "I know that's a lie," and I refuse to live my life changing course based on your lies. This requires sober-mindedness, especially as the leader of those who now know that they may be under threat for continuing the mission. The temptation was to put it "on pause" for a little bit while things “cool off.’ Or perhaps he was tempted to take a big old army so that he couldn't be ambushed and go out to meet with them to see if he could smooth things over. But he remains clear-headed and not driven or steered by their demands and threats and lies. He holds his course. *In Your Life * He doesn’t wring his hands and get worried because someone's getting ready to lie about him. Instead, he trusts God and refuses to be cowed by their slander. This kind of threatening and sabotage has the main goal of trying to get under your skin and make you worry. And if you let it, you're letting them win. We need to take Jesus at his word when he says that we should **rejoice ** when we are reviled. It's common for Christians to want to keep a good reputation, especially with the outside world and unbelievers, so badly that they are willing to tone down their conviction or change course. They are hoping that toning things down will help them not be perceived as politically incorrect or intolerant. What they are missing is that being "respectable" to your enemy comes at the cost of being tolerable to the enemy. In order to be respectable to your enemy, you have to convince them that you aren't *really * enemies, you aren’t actually dead-set against each other. And that's a lie. Light has no fellowship with darkness. When darkness decides to lie and cheat and slander you, you can't respond by living by their lies. First and foremost, you can’t do that because that's not living a life with integrity. Our Lord said that he is the way, the truth, and the life. Following him means we can't ignore what the truth is if it will give us a better reputation with those who don't like the truth.

It's also just a losing and unwise strategy. When it's non-negotiable to you to keep a good reputation with your enemies, or at least to do everything you can to keep a good reputation with your enemies, you are fighting to control something that simply isn't in your control. We can't control what others do or say. We need to trust God with what we cannot control and live with righteousness and integrity before God. When darkness finds the light blinding, we shouldn't be surprised. Your enemies will take your good desire to be thought well of and turn it against you. They purposely construe your actions in the worst possible light. They purposely misunderstand you. This slander attack comes in all kinds of ways. The feminized world we live in attempts to make ambitious young men slow down. They suggest that their ambition is really pride or greed. That's taking a good desire to take dominion and misconstruing it to change a young man's behavior. Wives, perhaps you’ve heard someone say about your submission to your husband, "It sounds to me like you're just being a pushover." That's taking your good desire to be a thoughtful individual and purposely misunderstanding it as though that's at odds with submitting to your husband. An extended family member who is upset that you didn't attend an event that was important to them might say with the rest of the family around, "It's because you just don't care about me." This is taking your good desire to have a loving relationship with them and a good name with your family and turning it against you. An employee at work might suggest that you are a domineering or out-of-touch boss, and everybody says so. This takes your good desire to have a loving connection with your employees and turns it against you. What all these have in common is that they take your desire for a good name and twist it so that it becomes motivation. So what does it look like to follow Nehemiah’s example when you are threatened with slander? 1. Trust the God of Truth. The worst-case scenario is that the truth comes out before the throne of God and you are vindicated there. 2. Do not live by lies. Don't change your behavior or decisions to accommodate this kind of slander terrorism. 3. Tell the person trying to manipulate you that you understand what they are doing. Expose the lie. 4. Make sure everyone who is on your side understands the slander is a lie and encourage them to continue with the mission. Pray for God to strengthen their hands. Attempt to Discredit. "They are coming to kill you by night"

The Attack Something I admire about Nehemiah is that his enemies understand that they are not going to be able to make him compromise on God, and so they try to turn his fear of God against him. Be the kind of man or woman that, when somebody decides they need to take you down, they try to weaponize your faith against you. The way that Sanballat, Tobiah, and Geshem try to do this is by bribing the prophets. They use their money and power and influence, and maybe threat of violence, to gain influence over someone that Nehemiah trusts. When they can't produce the leverage that they want, they go to someone closer to him and try to get the leverage that way. This is the same basic strategy that every kid knows: when Dad says no, they go to mom, and try to make her feel really bad for them. If they can succeed, then mom will go convince dad to give in. What they're aiming to do is discredit Nehemiah. The prophet tries to get him to demonstrate he's a coward by running away instead of standing to fight, and he tries to get him to compromise in his faith by disobeying God and hiding in a section of the temple he's not supposed to be in. If Nehemiah had listened to this prophet, he would have lost the respect of his men. Who wants to stand and fight behind a leader who runs away? He would have lost his moral authority: who can claim to be the leader of God's people when he doesn't even obey and respect God enough to obey his house rules? They already tried to undermine Nehemiah's leadership by writing that open letter that was supposed to create pressure so that people wouldn't keep building because they were scared of what the King might do. Now they're looking to undermine Nehemiah's leadership by undermining his character. If they can publicly demonstrate that he's not the great man that he looks to be, the people won't follow him the way that they've been following him. This is the most dangerous kind of attack. If it were successful, Nehemiah's ability to lead would be hampered forever. It would cut his legs out from underneath him. *Nehemiah’s Response * But Nehemiah sees through their trap again. The prophet has an initially plausible message. He says that they are coming to kill Nehemiah by night. That's easy to believe, but his instructions aren't those of a true prophet. His instructions are to run away and hide in the temple, which of course Nehemiah is not supposed to be in. It looks like he is suggesting going all the way into the temple, into the holy place, closing the door where he might be safer. There is no technique here that saves Nehemiah. It is his fear of God that saves him. He would rather die than disown God or be a coward. He says in verse 11, "Should a man such as I run away? What

man such as I could go into the temple and live? I will not go in." He understands his role as a leader does not allow him to run away from trouble, and he does not follow God merely when it is convenient. He follows him even unto death. Nehemiah sees what the whole plan is in verse 13. The goal is to give him a bad name so that they could taunt him and undermine his leadership, but he demonstrates he's not tauntable. He won't act in such a way to have a bad name. They'll have to lie about him to give him a bad name, and then he prays to God again and asks God to remember his enemies who tried to make him afraid and so to sin. He doesn't take vengeance into his own hands. Instead, he trusts God to deal with his enemies. *In Your Life * This attack to discredit you is what's happening often when others try to provoke you to sin. Especially when someone who is hierarchically "lower" than someone else, like a child to a parent, and they aim to provoke their authority, what they're trying to do is remove some of their moral authority by causing them to sin. A child might provoke their father to anger because they know their father cannot consistently punish their whining or insubordination if he's just lost his temper. A wife may try to provoke her husband to sin so that he doesn't have the authority to confront her for her own sins. An employee might try to publicly trap their boss in their words so that they're stuck with the decision they didn't try to make. ⠀ As with the other two attacks, there's no special technique or secret knowledge to defeat attempts to discredit you. Nehemiah comes out of the attack unscathed because he is a man of untainted character. He doesn't take the bait. Of course, he didn't want to die by assassination at night, but even more than he didn't want to die, he didn't want to be a coward or disown God. The fear of God is a shield against compromise for him, and can be for us as well. When we fear God more than we fear bad consequences man can throw our way, we become unsabotageable, unsteerable, unmanipulatable. The reason is that the fear of God says, “I will follow Jesus, come what may, no matter what I am walking by the light of His commands. I will not turn off this straight and narrow for anything or anyone.” Every attempt at sabotage is ultimately trying to disorder your heart by taking some good desire, like a desire for a good name or to be cooperative, and twist that desire by pitting it against your fear for God and obedience and allegiance to him. That's why the glorious God-Given antidote is the sober-mindedness that comes from fearing God. Conclusion

In conclusion, learn with me from Nehemiah’s example to be sober-minded in the midst of sabatage. Don’t be fooled by dishonest diplomacy, or threats of slander, or attempts to discredit you. Fear God, and walk in his ways. Fear him so deeply that you’d rather die than disown him. Then the fear of God will be your anchor, and give you the steadiness of soul to be sober-minded. Amen. Heavenly father, we know that you are without parts or passions. And so you are always sober- minded. You are never drawn aside by your feelings or tricked by your enemies. And we praise you for it. Please help us to imitate you in it as far as it is appropriate and possible for us as your finite creatures. Help us to be sober-minded. Amen.
