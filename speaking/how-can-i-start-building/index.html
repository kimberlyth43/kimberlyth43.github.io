How Can I Start Building and Fighting?

Nehemiah 1:1–2:8

Preached by Nathan Colestock — July 20, 2025

Christ the King Church · Stillwater, MN

BUILD BY FAITH

You know that our strategy is to build and fight. I hope by now you find it exciting and inspiring. But perhaps you are still wondering, “How do I start building? What am I supposed to do when I see an opportunity to build?” Wouldn’t it be helpful if we had an example we could look at and say, “Imitate that!”? That is exactly what we have in Nehemiah 1:1–2:10.

At the beginning of Nehemiah, it is the twentieth year of the king—about thirteen years after the events of Ezra. The temple had been completed in four years, between the second and sixth years of the king. By now there should have been significant progress in Jerusalem. And there was. In Ezra 9:9, Ezra thanks God for “a wall in Judah and Jerusalem.” Some translations render it “protection” because they take it as a metaphor. But when Hanani comes to Nehemiah in chapter 1 with the news that the city is broken down and its gates burned with fire, it seems clear that real progress had been made, only for the enemies of God to attack and destroy what had been rebuilt. That is why the remnant is in great trouble and shame.

Every good story needs a problem to solve, a mountain to climb, an enemy to defeat. In Ezra the central story was the rebuilding of the temple so that worship could be reestablished in the land. In Nehemiah the story is the rebuilding of the city and its walls so that God would be honored in the land.

Because God has staked his glory and his word on his people, their trouble and shame reflect on him. When they were sent into exile, their shame displayed God’s justice and thereby showed his glory. But now that they have returned and are seeking to serve him—however imperfectly—their continued shame and trouble may cause the nations to wonder whether God will keep his promises to restore them. After all, he had said in Deuteronomy 4, “The Lord will scatter you among the peoples … but from there you will seek the Lord your God, and you will find him.”

Nehemiah longs to see God’s promise fulfilled. He wants to build and fight so that God’s glory is clearly seen in the fortunes of his people. And the way he begins is both an example and an inspiration to us. Like Paul, he says in effect, “Follow me as I follow Christ.” We can follow Nehemiah as a model of how to start building and fighting.

  • He remains loyal to God in exile.
  • He confesses his sins. He does not shift blame or feel better by comparison, but leans into God’s word.
  • He trusts in God’s word.
  • He prays and asks for God’s help.
  • He makes a detailed, wise plan.
  • He courageously acts.

STAY LOYAL

Let’s begin with Nehemiah’s example of staying loyal to God in exile. When he receives word that the remnant is in trouble and shame, he mourns. Verse 4 tells us he mourned “for days.”

This is how we see his loyalty. He still cared deeply for the mission of God and the remnant of his people. He did not adapt his values to his surroundings. He did not become a man of Susa while living in Susa. He kept his loyalty to God’s people. He ached over their setbacks. When God’s people suffered shame, he did not make light of it. He had risen to a high position—cupbearer to the king. He could have settled into a comfortable life and forgotten the remnant. But he did not. His heart was gripped by God’s glory, and he knew that Jerusalem must be rebuilt for the sake of that glory. So he wept when the work was in danger.

The first step in building and fighting is to cultivate a deep, personal, unshakable loyalty to God. At the root, this is not the work of the will but the work of God. He creates in us a new heart. He gives us a new spirit, a heart of flesh instead of stone. If Christ is not yet your King, if your heart is still stony and cold toward him and his ways, pray to the Father. Ask him for the gift of repentance. Turn from unbelief, trust Christ, and receive a heart that is soft to his voice, eager to obey, and broken over its sin.

Even after God has given you a new heart, you must guard it. “Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life” (Proverbs 4:23).

Too many in our day have given in to the passions of our nation. Their instincts no longer burn to fight for Christ. They abandon brothers and sisters who stand for unpopular truth. Their devotion has shifted to typical American sensibilities. Instead of being salt and light, they hide their light and lose their saltiness. We might call this “going native.” They live in the land and become part of it rather than remaining a counter-culture. They trade their loyalty for the comfort of fitting in.

One temptation is to feel embarrassed by how different your Christian life looks from the world around you. The operating system of our culture is entertainment. We try to gamify everything—education, work, even religion. The result is shallow devotion and a life spent as a constant spectator.

This “god is my belly” approach trivializes everything it touches. In the 1980s Neil Postman wrote Amusing Ourselves to Death. Even from a secular vantage point he saw that our obsession with passive consumption and amusement would ruin us. His point was that in America everything has become a performance. Politics, news, even church services must be “engaging”—which is often code for entertaining.

Having fun and being engaged is not the problem. But when we make entertainment our god and indulge that appetite too far, our loyalties drift toward the most interesting screen in the moment or the favorite talking heads on that screen.

Nehemiah’s first example to us is his tears. His tears reveal his loyalty. What disappoints you? Do your disappointments show loyalty to God, or to something else? If your building and fighting do not flow from a heart loyal to God, it will come to nothing. Worse, you will be building your own competing kingdom instead of advancing God’s. Test yourself, that you may be in the world but not of it.

TAKE RESPONSIBILITY

When Nehemiah opens his mouth in prayer, the first thing out is confession: “Even I and my father’s house have sinned.” He does not pretend he has been innocent of the problem that led to this shame. He takes responsibility.

This is not a perfunctory prayer. He spent days in prayer, fasting, and mourning. The prayer recorded here is a summary of those days—and actually of months—of prayer before he appeared sorrowful before the king.

As a quick aside before we consider the importance of confession, notice the days Nehemiah spends mourning, praying, and fasting. Because we can have almost anything we want with next-day shipping, we have lost the pace of heart required for fasting and prayer. There is no drive-through window for the spiritual life. God did not design our hearts to run at microprocessor speed. He did not even design them to run at the pace of our busy minds. That is why we can intellectually grasp a situation long before it truly sinks in and “hits” us. To live wisely and wholeheartedly, we may need to create more space for reflection and prayer.

Often that kind of reflection enables us to take responsibility and offer a full confession rather than deflect blame. It would have been easy for Nehemiah to say, “They are not turning to God with their hearts,” or to compare himself: “I am not as bad as them, Lord. After all, they intermarried!”

Reformation begins with confession. When we stop pointing only at others, when we stop blaming God, we acknowledge that we are part of the problem. Nehemiah knows that God’s judgment has come because his people “have acted corruptly against you and have not kept your commandments” (1:7). Sin is not an abstract legal category somewhere out in the cosmos. Sin is a relational rebellion that creates separation between unholy us and holy God. God hates sin. Therefore the first step in building and fighting is dealing with our own sin.

As you prepare to rebuild ruins that seem beyond repair, first acknowledge the role you have played in the destruction.

  • If your marriage has grown rocky, begin by confessing every sin you can name. Do not shift blame to your spouse. Do not compare yourself to other husbands or wives to feel better. Take responsibility and confess.
  • If your children are resisting your authority, first confess your own sins—perhaps of anger or impatience. Do not misuse your parental authority by constantly criticizing them. Do not compare yourself to other parents and grow proud. Confess your sins.

This principle holds for large-scale reformation and revival as well. If we want to see a change from the trivial, half-hearted, entertainment-focused, compromised worship so common today, we must first acknowledge our own part. We have trivialized God. We have stayed silent when we should have spoken. We have been willfully ignorant because we wanted to go along with the crowd.

TRUST GOD’S WORD

Notice, however, the hope that undergirds Nehemiah’s confession. In verse 5 he reminds himself and God that he is “the great and awesome God who keeps covenant and steadfast love with those who love him and keep his commandments.” That may sound odd coming from a man about to confess that he and his people have acted corruptly and have not kept the commandments. What good is God’s steadfast love for commandment-keepers when we are commandment-breakers?

Nehemiah has studied the Scriptures and knows there is still hope. God had promised that if his people returned to him, he would gather them from the lands of exile and dwell with them again. At the beginning of this sermon I quoted Deuteronomy 4. Here is the same promise again from Deuteronomy 30, after God has laid out the blessings for obedience and the curses for rebellion.

Read Deuteronomy 30:1–5.

Nehemiah takes God at his word and trusts it with specificity. He does not pray, “Lord, maybe this return was not meant to be and we misunderstood the Scriptures. Perhaps you will act now, or perhaps in another generation.” He does not turn God’s concrete, specific promises into something abstract and general.

Many have observed that it is easy to love humanity in the abstract. What is hard is to love your actual brother or sister in the specific circumstances God has given. In the same way, faith in the abstract is easy. What is difficult is to trust God with specificity. This is why trials are so valuable. They force us to trust God with specificity. When money is tight, you do not merely “trust God to provide.” You pray earnestly for him to provide specific work this month so the rent can be paid, and you trust him to do it. When a child wanders, you do not simply “trust God to take care of them.” You plead with him to bring them back, grant them repentance, and restore their faith.

Beneath all our lives, once God has given us new hearts, there should be a general loyalty: “I will do anything you say, Lord.” But like Nehemiah, that general faith must become specific in two ways.

First, it must get specific with God’s word. Notice that Nehemiah quotes God’s own words back to him in verse 9. He trusts specific promises. Do not trust the Bible generally. Dive in. Find promises, teachings, and doctrines to build your life on. Stake your marriage on “Be slow to anger, quick to listen, slow to speak.” Stake your parenting on “Whoever spares the rod hates his son.” Stake your business on “The way of the wicked will perish.”

Second, it must get specific in what it asks for. Nehemiah does not merely trust the passage; he interprets it rightly and applies it wisely. In verse 10 he identifies the remnant as the very servants and people God has promised to gather and dwell with. He looks at God’s word, then trusts God to fulfill it in the real world right in front of him.

So Nehemiah had a specific promise, trusted it in the real world, and asked God to do something specific for the remnant in Jerusalem. What promise should we trust? All of them. If you are working to rebuild personal discipline, holiness, marriage, parenting, or vocation, ransack God’s word. Trust it and pray specifically. As a church, as we seek to build and fight, I hope you will join me in trusting the Great Commission.

Our Lord said, “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age.”

Here Jesus gives us two promises and our marching orders. The orders are to make disciples by baptizing and teaching. The promises are at the beginning and the end: Jesus has all authority, and Jesus is with us always. How can we trust this with specificity?

Mothers, sometime tomorrow you will be inundated with requests from your children. You will be trying to remember the grocery list, thinking about how many places to set at the table, and wondering how so much rice is still stuck to the floor. You will be tempted to impatience or despair. But this passage gives you everything you need. Being a mother is making disciples of your own children. You are serving your Savior—the one who has all authority and has promised to be with you. So you can trust that he is present and helping you. You can respond to your children with love, teach them by example to count it all joy when they face trials, and press on in obedience because you are glad Jesus has authority over you. You can trust that God will give you everything you need that afternoon to love your children, teach them, and put dinner on the table for your guests.

Gentlemen, tomorrow you will arrive at work. Your inbox will be full. You will face a difficult problem. In the back of your mind you will be hoping for a raise to ease financial pressure and provide for your household. You will be trying to leave in time to take your kids to their evening activity. You will be tempted to anger, impatience, or discontentment. But again this passage gives you everything you need. To make disciples in all of life, someone must show the world what God-honoring supply chains, financial structures, delivery services, restaurant hospitality, and construction look like. You are there to make disciples and teach them to obey everything Christ has commanded. Even if opportunities to proclaim the gospel verbally are rare, you are teaching obedience to Christ through honest business practices and a hard day’s work. You are one of his ambassadors in that company. So you can trust him for that day. You can trust him to be present and give you grace in a difficult meeting. You can trust that the “all authority” of Jesus includes your boss when you are mistreated, and you can continue without bitterness.

As a church we can trust that God wants us here in the Stillwater area. We can trust that he means for us to stay and therefore trust him for a permanent location. We can trust God to prepare a building for us to worship in because we have seen clear evidence that he wants us here making disciples.

ASK FOR GOD’S HELP

Nehemiah not only trusts God’s word; he asks God for his help. He does not imagine he is smart enough, strong enough, or good enough to handle this on his own. He knows he needs God. So he asks. In verse 11 you see him pleading with God to be attentive to his prayer. In fact, he has recruited others to pray with him.

There are at least two reasons we often skip prayer. The first is self-confidence; we do not think we need God’s help. The second is lack of specific faith; we do not really believe God cares about us or listens to us.

Church, do you believe God hears your prayers and delights in you? That he cares about you specifically, not merely about his people in general? That he is orchestrating and intervening in your life for your good and the good of others? He does. He proves it over and over. He proves it doctrinally: he chose us before the foundation of the world. He proves it poetically: he knows the number of hairs on our heads, not one sparrow falls without his knowledge, he clothes the grass of the field, and he values us far more than birds or lilies. And he proves it personally. You are here, after all.

MAKE A PLAN

Nehemiah does not rush into the king’s presence by the seat of his pants. He has a plan. It is remarkably specific. He intends to leverage his position as cupbearer and the skills that made him successful in that role. He has thought about it for months between Hanani’s visit and the month of Nisan. He has departure and return dates in mind. He knows which letters he needs. He is ready to move the moment God gives him favor with the king.

He is not praying a vague “Please do something, Lord.” He has a day, a plan, a materials list—everything that can be prepared in advance—so that when the king gives the green light, he is ready. Because Nehemiah has specific faith, he can make specific plans and trust God to bless them.

Andy taught us last week a biblical framework for decision-making:

  • Holy Desire
  • Open Door
  • Wise Counsel
  • Biblical Wisdom

Notice how Nehemiah follows this pattern. When he hears Jerusalem is in ruins, he has a holy desire to help rebuild. His plans leverage his position as cupbearer. He has open access to the king. He has recruited others to pray with him—presumably to plan and assist as well—giving him wise counsel. And he is soaked in biblical wisdom drawn from the promises of God.

You can apply this both to where to build and fight—changing jobs, investing in people, starting businesses—and to making concrete plans for success in those areas.

Nehemiah did not plan so little that he could say, “Well, God, you will just have to make it work.” Most of the time God does not work the way he did with Gideon’s three hundred, causing the enemy to turn on itself. Most of the time God works through well-laid plans—like David storing materials for the temple, or Ezra preparing for years before leading the people back to worship. Nehemiah planned enough to say, “Lord, I have done everything I can think to do. Now please prosper this plan.” He made plans with a reasonable hope of success and asked God to bless them.

I believe this kind of planning is one of the ways God will transform this area—through our brainstorming, praying, planning, and executing. I know some of you are planning to move closer. Some are planning business or career changes. Some are planning to strengthen your marriage and parenting. See in Nehemiah that this kind of godly planning is exactly what you should be doing. As you plan, you are exercising biblical wisdom and taking dominion. It is precisely what God calls you to.

The Bible says, “The heart of man plans his way, but the Lord establishes his steps.” It also says, “Unless the Lord builds the house, those who build it labor in vain.” But that does not mean the builders can stay home and the house will appear. It means the work and the planning and the execution all require God’s blessing.

As you plan and choose which opportunities to seize, pay close attention to the station God has given you. Nehemiah was cupbearer to the king. That was not an accidental role. In that day it was common to attempt to assassinate a king by poison. The cupbearer tasted everything first. He therefore had strong motivation to ensure the entire kitchen and supply chain were free of treachery. As one of the most trusted men at court, he carried significant authority because the king believed he had the king’s best interest at heart.

There is a biblical principle here: authority flows toward integrity and responsibility.

COURAGEOUSLY ACT

Finally, all the faith, prayer, and planning would have accomplished nothing if Nehemiah had not had the courage to act. He could chart the course, but he would go nowhere unless he had the courage to set sail.

Do not underestimate how crucial this final step is. Many pray and plan, but when the moment for decisive, courageous action arrives, they shrink back. Nehemiah was afraid (2:2). He was risking his life. Appearing disloyal to the king could have meant a horrible death. You may wonder why he was so afraid. The cupbearer was a powerful position, often carrying Joseph-like authority. His request could easily have been interpreted as preparation for rebellion or the establishment of a rival capital. That is why his first words reaffirm his loyalty to the king before he makes his request.

When he says he was “very much afraid,” we should take him at his word. If the king suspected treason, Nehemiah would die—likely after torture. It is a mark of his loyalty and faith that he found courage to speak in the face of such consequences.

Nehemiah shows us that building and fighting with God includes accepting risk. As John Piper has said, risk is right. Trust God, make a good plan, ask him to bless it, and then act.

CONCLUSION AND APPLICATION

So Nehemiah gives us a clear example of how to get our building and fighting off the ground.

  • He remains loyal to God in exile.
  • He confesses his sins. He does not shift blame or feel better by comparison, but leans into God’s word.
  • He trusts in God’s word.
  • He prays and asks for God’s help.
  • He makes a detailed, wise plan.
  • He courageously acts.

Whatever you are planning to build next, follow the wisdom God has set before us in Nehemiah.

  • Perhaps you are trying to build a financially stable household and are not sure where to begin. Start by taking responsibility. Believe that God will care for you. Ask him for wisdom. Create a budget, set spending goals, pray over the plan, and then work hard and live thriftily.
  • If you are unhappy in your marriage, start by taking responsibility. Confess your sins to God and to your spouse. Pray for God’s help, search the Scriptures, seek mentors, and ask counsel from those who are doing well. Once you have a plan you believe God will bless, ask him to bless it—and then get to work.
  • If you have grown stagnant in your work or career, take responsibility for where you have settled. Ask whether this honors God, your family, and your employer. Search the Scriptures to recover God’s vision for your labor. Make a plan, list the commitments you will keep, and then act.
  • Maybe you look at large problems in the world—increasing crime, foolish policies—and you want to see change. Confess any part you have played. Consider where you are in life and what steps would be required to gain a position of meaningful influence. Pray for God’s blessing, make a plan, and begin to build.

I hope Nehemiah’s example and these six steps make building and fighting less abstract and more concrete. All you need is:

  • A ruin to rebuild
  • A heart to obey
  • A humble spirit
  • Faith in God’s word
  • Time to pray
  • A plan to follow
  • And the courage to start.

As we go, we know that our small efforts are not decisive. They are taken up and blessed by God as we are swept along in the rushing tide of his purpose to make Christ preeminent in all things.

Amen.

PRAYER

Heavenly Father, please convict us where we have been disloyal to you. Give us humility to admit our failures rather than blame others. Help us to trust your word. Be attentive to our prayers and bless our plans to glorify you in our lives, our households, and our community. By your kindness give us courage to act on wise plans. And in all of it, may you be glorified in every part of life. Amen.
