Put Your Name on Something

Nehemiah 3

Preached by Nathan Colestock — November 16, 2025

Christ the King Church · Stillwater, MN

Introduction

If you go to many war memorials you will find a list of the names of those being memorialized. The function of the memorial is to help us remember their sacrifice, but also to inspire us to action.

Last week I was in Washington DC for a church planting conference. While I was there I visited the new National World War I Memorial. It was sobering and inspiring. The figures of the men fighting capture the desperation and their belief in the cause. On the back of the monument is an excerpt from a poem by Archibald MacLeish, a WWI veteran.

"Whether our lives and our deaths were for peace and a new hope, or for nothing, we cannot say. It is you who must say this. They say: we leave you our deaths. Give them their meaning."

The monument is meant to inspire those who visit to make, by their living, the sacrifice of those who died worthwhile. To create the civilization of peace and hope they fought for a reality. That's the effect this list of names from Nehemiah has on me. This list of names comes as a kind of grand accomplishment. It's reminiscent of the mighty-men list from Samuel. It recounts their great struggle to do what God gave them to do despite the hardship and danger. They spurned discouragement and pain and with God's help accomplished something magnificent.

My aim today is that this list of names would have that effect on you. I want you to leave praying for God’s help to put your name on something. And so, toward that end, I’ll preach to you on this theme: God’s glory in Christian civilization.

I've got three arguments in this sermon. First, “Christian civilization is good.” In this section I’ll show you that the function of this list demonstrates the importance of Christian civilization. Then I’ll define what I mean by “Christian civilization” and give you two reasons that Christian civilization is good. My second argument is that “Christian civilization is in ruins.” In this section we’ll look around us to see how our Christian civilization is in ruins. Third, I’ll show you six lessons from Nehemiah 3 on how to build Christian civilization. Hidden in these names are treasures for us to put into practice.

Christian civilization is good.

First, Christian civilization is good. I'm not just pulling this point out because I like it (although I do). When we get a list of names like this, the most important question to ask is what it's doing here. For instance, think about the genealogy at the beginning of Matthew. What's most important isn't the particular names (although there’s some really interesting stuff there for those who dig). What's most important is the function of genealogy. In Matthew's case, it's to show that Jesus is the king of Judah, the blessing from Abraham, and the second Adam.

The literary function of this list of names.

So what's the point of this list? The point is to show the grandness, the greatness of the accomplishment of building the wall. Not only is their name listed, but also how much each person built.

There is another list structured like this in 2 Samuel 23. There the mighty men of David are listed by name and with their accomplishments.

“Jeshebbassebeth a Tehchemonite; he was chief of the three. He wielded his spear against eight hundred whom he killed at one time…next to him was Shammah, the son of Agee the Hararite. The Philistines gathered together at Lehi, where there was a plot of ground full of lentils, and the men fled from the Philistines. But he took his stand in the midst of the plot and defended it and struck down the Philistines, and the LORD worked a great victory.”

Do you see how this list in Nehemiah 3 works the same way? They go: name, provenance, and then accomplishment. The men are presented as a new set of mighty men who waged war in wall-building instead of by David's side. This list and one the ones that follow later in the book record for perpetuity the accomplishment of this group of returnee's.

Why is their accomplishment presented as so grand?

But that leaves me with another question: why do they think their work is so grand? Why are they like new mighty men? I mean, the wall was important and everything, but these people came from Susa. Compare the walls of Susa to these walls and it's no contest. These just aren't that impressive. We know from archeological evidence that the wall was really just thrown together with whatever stones they could find and from the rubble left after the destruction. It wasn't the work of professional masons. It was amateur work. So why did they think it was so impressive? Because of its trajectory. Because of the mission.

While Israel was in exile, Ezekiel wrote his great vision of a temple and a city. Fresh on their mind was Ezekiel 47. In that chapter the prophet sees water flowing out from the temple, through the courtyard and the city and out the gates of the city to the world. As the water continues out from the city it grows deeper and deeper. Around the river stand trees with good fruit. And the angel says (and just think about this symbolism for a moment),

"On the banks, on both sides of the river, there will grow all kinds of trees for food. Their leaves will not wither, nor their fruit fail, but they will bear fresh fruit every month, because the water for them flows from the sanctuary. Their fruit will be for food and their leaves for healing."

You don't need to be an expert to understand the symbolism here. Water flows from the temple, through the city to the world . It provides fresh water, good fruit from trees, and healing in the leaves of the trees. This is a vision of God transforming the world. He sends from his temple healing waters. First they travel through the city, and then to the world. He provides for them trees of life for sustenance and healing.

Their work is so grand because it’s the first step in the healing of the world.

This prophecy makes sense of why the Bible presents their work as so grand. From where Nehemiah sits, this is the first step in the healing of the world. He doesn’t expect an actual river to flow out of the temple. He understands that’s the grace and saving power of God. But there does need to be a civilization, a city on a hill, for that power to flow out of according to the vision.

Building the temple and city is such a grand accomplishment not because in itself it's so impressive but because it contributes to God's mission of redemption.

I have mentioned to you before the story of JFK visiting NASA. While he was there, he met a janitor and asked him, “So what is it you do here?” And the janitor said, “I’m putting a man on the moon.” He understood how his little piece fit into the grand mission, and so, his little role was filled with grand dignity. That’s what’s happening with this list. The specific outcome of their work isn’t all that stupendous. What’s great is the mission they are on: to build the civilization, the society, that God will use to send his living, healing water to the ends of the earth.

With the rest of this section I will define Christian civilization and then give you three reasons that Christian civilization is good.

Define Christian civilization.

Civilization is the high level of technology and culture that comes as a result of humans working together. Civilization emerges from generations of men and women working together to make a way of life. As such, a civilization is the manifestation of a culture. If you want to know what a culture is like, look at their cities. In cities, their values are cast in steel and stone. And, of course, we know that culture flows from worship.

So, people live together and worship God. They establish a God-honoring culture that finds specific ways to demonstrate joy and love and grace and celebrate the gifts of God. As time goes on, they will advance in technology and their culture will become thick enough that you’d look at their way of life and call it a “civilization.”

Civilization is the organized way of life, including technology, architecture, and customs, created by a culture advancing to a sufficient degree.

Now, what makes a civilization Christian? a civilization is Christian if it embodies Christ's values. A Christian civilization isn’t necessarily all Christian. That is, not everyone in a Christian city or Christian nation needs to be Christians to call it “Christian.” But conscious obedience to Christ as a people constitutes a Christian people.

Overlap with other terms.

To help situate us, let me differentiate this term “Christian civilization” from a couple other related terms: the Kingdom of God and Christendom.

The Kingdom of God is the rule and realm of God. Kings have both rule and realm; power and a place. In one sense, God’s kingdom includes everything, since he has all power and authority and dominion. But in another sense, the kingdom is coming and growing, just like Scripture says. Jesus’ parables do not describe the kingdom as a static, always infinite power of God. Instead, it grows and works through the world. Think of the parables where Jesus says the kingdom is like leaven working through a loaf. So, God’s kingdom is in one sense all that there is and in another sense, the places on earth where his will is done as it is in heaven.

The Kingdom of God is not the same as Christian Civilization. Christian Civilizations are manifestations of the kingdom of God because they are places where God’s will is done on earth. But the Kingdom of God is a much bigger category that in one sense includes all things and in another sense includes all Christian civilizations and anyplace, even if it’s just a household, where God’s will is being done faithfully.

Christendom is another overlapping term. The term “Christendom” is often used in two senses. First, it means the global church—all believers still alive on the earth. Someone might say “Christendom is growing” and they mean that the global church is growing. A second sense of “Christendom” is used by Wilson in his book Mere Christendom. There he advocates for a civilization that is given to Jesus in every sphere, especially politically. There's a lot of overlap in how Wilson uses the term Christendom and how I'm using the term "Christian civilization" in this sermon. However, "Christendom" often focuses on the political ramifications of having Christ as lord of a country (like including the Apostle’s Creed in the constitution, or Sabbath laws). I'm all about those ramifications applied in wisdom. But as I talk about Christian Civilization, I'm talking about way more than politics.

Christian civilization is the fruit of generations and generations of Christian obedience. It takes thousands and thousands of faithful moms and dads. Tens of thousands of kids honoring their parents. It takes thousands of moms who create the culture around holidays by making sure there’s gingerbread men to be dentally decapitated around Christmas, and ham to eat over Easter, and the “Happy Birthday” sign is hung in celebration of God’s image born into a home. It takes thousands of men committed to serving their community at great cost to themselves. Men who pour out their lives to roof houses, run electrical wiring, manage employees, found organizations, and serve the body politic.

Over time, people's intelligence increases as they become better at educating their children. Their morality increases as they learn to parent more faithfully. They grow in knowledge of the Bible as their pastors minister more effectively. They develop helpful patterns of celebration and thanksgiving around holidays. They establish normal household rhythms that most follow. They create new technologies that buy back more time to turn a profit on.

Christian civilization is what happens when all of life is taken captive to Christ for generations and God blesses their efforts.

Here’s two reasons (as if we needed any evidence!) that a civilization like that is good.

Two reasons Christian civilization is good.

1. Christian civilization is part of God's design for humanity.

God designed humans to create civilizations. He made us so that civilization is inevitable. You can create cities and technologies that incarnate a God-honoring culture or that incarnate our wicked hatred for God. But there’s no way to avoid civilization. God is a community in himself, three in one as the Trinity. He made us to live together in community to demonstrate what he is like.

God did not make us "noble savages" who are better on our own. That was Rousseau's idea in the Enlightenment era. And it was beaten into the American consciousness by the transcendentalist writers who wrote as though if we just walked away from civilization and joined them out at Walden pond everything would be better. Instead of believing the biblical doctrine that we are born sinful and that civilization often restrains our wickedness, they believed that we were good or at least neutral and that it is civilization that made us wicked. But they are wrong. Human culture and civilization is good. We don’t mess up everything we touch. Earth would not be better off without us. God put us here to complete and glorify earth by working together. God prepared the earth for us to civilize. He put gold in the earth for us to dig up. He pressed diamonds for us to learn to work them. He stashed oceans of natural gas for us to harness.

Civilization is so natural to how God made us that even in our fallen state, it took only zero generations to plant fields and cultivate livestock. It only took seven generations to go from skin garments to tents, livestock, musical instruments, bronze and even iron tools. Think about that! Adam was still alive when someone made the first iron plow and strummed the first lyre. God didn’t create the world hoping that we wouldn't inhabit it. He made it for us to tame, to take dominion over. God designed humans to civilize earth.

But Rousseau's ideas are still alive and well in today's conservative movement. It’s the underlying assumption behind the conservative tendency to retreat and get away and try to be alone. “That civilization will infect me and my family. If we can just get away then we’ll be safe.” Well, civilization will infect you. It may pull your family down. But getting away isn’t the solution. You’ll find plenty of sin in your own hearts even if you retreat. The solution God has provided for us in Christ is a new society, a new civilization: the church of God that builds one another up.

We want to retreat from the civilization that secularism has made because in it we are like fish out of water. But that doesn’t mean civilization is bad. Our cities encapsulate our values as a people. In Lord of the Rings, the Elven cities are works of art. They harmoniously live in nature while making a comfortable civilization. The dwarves make another kind of civilization under the mountains that is wholly different but still grand and impressive. Of course secularism makes a sad civilization. The question we should ask is, “What kind of civilization will we build? How will we capture our love and joy and peace? How will we put hospitality in brick and mortar?”

In summary, Christian civilization is good because God designed humans to form civilizations.

2. Christian civilization is part of God's design for redemption.

A second argument that Christian civilization is is that it’s part of God’s design for redemption.

This point is not a way to smuggle post-millenialism in for everyone. As far as I know, I’m the only pastor here who is post-mil, and would never try to just smuggle it in hidden in a sermon.

Here are two areas where it’s biblically clear that Christian civilization is key to God’s redeeming grace.

1. God receives the fruit of our civilization as offerings.

God has used and keeps using elements of our civilization as pieces of his redemption. This is really astonishing. He doesn’t ask us to bring rocks or trees back to him. He asks for the fruits of civilization. It started at the very beginning when Cain and Abel both brought the fruits of their civilizing efforts (livestock and agriculture). God continues using the fruits of civilization in his acts of redemption. He receives more sacrifices from civilization, creates a stylized tent (tabernacle) inlaid with gold and whose instruments are the work of master craftsmen. His temple makes even more use of the efforts of human civilization. He gives us his word, in human language and writing systems. And now he feeds us with bread and wine, neither of which exist in the natural world.

God planned to use human civilization to communicate to us his plan of redemption. He continues to use it each week as we come to worship him.

2. God shows that redemption flows from Christian civilization.

A second reason that Christian civilization is good is that God says that his redeeming grace flows from Christian civilizations. God gives us not one, but two great prophetic images of the world being renewed by his grace where that grace flows out from a city—the symbol of civilization. We’ve already discussed Ezekiel’s vision where water flows from the temple through the city to the ends of the earth.

In Revelation we see a New Jerusalem coming down out of heaven. It’s presented as the height of civilization. It’s decked with rubies and jasper and emeralds. But in this city there is no temple. “For the temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb.” The city has no temple because God dwells in the whole city with its inhabitants. Remember, this city, the New Jerusalem is the bride of the Lamb. It’s us, the church. In the following verses, Ezekiel’s vision is more or less repeated. The nations walk by the light of the city. The kings of the earth bring their glory and honor into it. The river of life flows out from the throne of God through the city to the world with trees bearing fruit and whose leaves are for the healing of the nations. In John 7 Jesus says that rivers of living water will flow from him for the world. Then John clarifies that this means the Holy Spirit. But now the Holy Spirit has come and is us. We are the vehicle that the living water comes to the world through.

Christ’s church is the vehicle through which God’s power and grace flows to the nations so that their kings bring their glory to God. You can believe that happens gradually now (post-mil), cataclysmically in a future millennium (pre-mil), or generally on the new earth (a-mil). But no matter what we are the society, the city that brings God’s blessings to the world for their healing.

It’s not by accident that God chooses a city to picture how his grace comes to the world through us. Christian civilization is implied by the church. If you gather Christians and they live faithfully together, they will form a distinct culture and eventually their own civilization living within the nations of the world. I believe this is exactly what Jesus has in mind when he says that the Kingdom of God is like leaven working through a loaf or a mustard seed that will grow. While it’s small, right at the beginning, it looks like the New Testament church. So what happens when it’s had a chance to work through the world? Traditions form, culture builds, holidays are established, integrity is enshrined in law, and before long, you have a Christian civilization. We, the church, are here to be a new humanity (and remember civilization is inescapable for humanity). Rather than being separated by national boundaries, like Israel, we are set apart by the sacraments: baptism and the Lord’s Supper. We are those washed clean by his grace. We are those who eat with God. Set free from the Old Adam, we get to make a new way of life based on the life, death, and accomplishment of the New Adam.

Our Christian civilization is in ruins.

Unfortunately, our civilization, which was founded as a city on a hill to shine the light of Christ into a dark world, is in ruins. You don’t need much evidence for this, but I’ll just mention two categories that concern me.

1. We are morally compromised.

American Christianity is awfully compromised.

  • We’re compromised by greed and materialism.
  • We’re compromised by fear of man.
  • We’re compromised by irreverence.
  • We’re compromised by moral slip. And this is perhaps the most dangerous. Many don’t know how far they are from God’s good standards. I recently sat at a coffee shop working. Next to me sat two young ladies. They were lamenting the awful plight of their friend. You see, her boyfriend had convinced her not to wear her cute mini skirt for Halloween (the only night of the year you can do that, apparently) and to dress up as something else with him. He was supposedly being controlling and they felt bad for her. Then, without flinching, they pulled out their Bibles and turned to 1 Timothy for their Bible Study.

Moral compromise is insidious because we probably don’t see it. So be vigilant to raise children with tender consciences that earnestly desire to please God.

2. We have low capacities.

Our potential is lower than our forefathers. If you want to be humbled this afternoon, go looking for late 1800’s and early 1900’s 8th grade exams. You probably couldn’t pass. As a society, our technology certainly advanced. But you’d be hard-pressed to convince yourself that the average person is smarter than they were 100 years ago.

Our Bible literacy is at an all-time low. Most Christians aren’t concerned. They think Google can answer the Bible questions and find that verse they were thinking about.

Our work ethic is tragically underwhelming. What would the Puritans have done with as much free time as most of us have available to us?

We don't need to be discouraged by this. God will help us. It also creates a marvelous opportunity for us. Education being what it is, our country will soon face a serious crisis of competency. The ability of the average worker will have fallen so drastically, lots of work will get worse. We have a chance to show the glory of God by offering goods and services that are in another league than our competitors. We have the opportunity to rise quickly through the ranks of our company to influence them for good.

Still, our Christian civilization is in ruins. And God put us here. It’s ours to rebuild. So, let’s learn from these mighty men how to rebuild a Christian civilization.

Learn from these mighty men to rebuild Christian civilization.

Here are six lessons for us to learn about building Christian civilization that we can learn from these mighty men listed in Nehemiah 3.

1. Build and fight to win.

This whole list comes as the response to the wicked rulers of the land. The last thing Nehemiah told their enemies is that God’s people will build the city, God will make them prosper, and the wicked will have no portion in it. They are in a competition, a near war with the enemies of God and building and fighting to win. So they roll up their sleeves. This list is like the crescendo to the end of the piece. Maybe it’d help to imagine this scene as a movie. Nehemiah tells them “We will rebuild this city! And you will have no right or claim here!” This list is the building montage. They get to work!

Don't box as one beating the air. You've got work to do. This is why godly ambition is so important. Your problem is probably not that you are too ambitious, but that you aren't ambitious enough. There is territory to be gained, markets to win, real-estate to own, and talented employees to attract. And in all those endeavors, as everywhere, God makes all the difference.

There’s a great quote from Rebecca Merkle that came across my wife’s daily calendar recently,

“No matter what house God has given us, our job is to throw ourselves at it and turn a profit. To work, to run, to build, to glorify, and to transform. If we actually did this, we would discover that we will never really run out of scope.”

There’s no end of scope. Keep building!

2. Build as an offering.

Look at it in 3:1. The first one to begin the building is the high priest. When he completed his work, they consecrated it as a kind of first-fruits offering. When the wall is complete, they will dedicate it to God as well. That’s significant. A wall isn’t a holy space. They were doing normal “secular” work but they dedicated it to God. This is how we need to learn to think about everything we do.

In one of Wilson’s books, he comments that if we are living sacrifices, then everywhere we are is an altar. Monday morning, your desk is an altar. Monday night, your dinner table is an altar. Whatever you do, you do as an offering to God.

So as you do your part to build Christian civilization for God, give it to him as an offering. Give him the Thanksgiving dinner, the family gathering, the sour dough, the spreadsheet, the sales pitch, all of it. As you do, it will:

  1. Increase your integrity. You won’t feel right selling someone hard on a product they don’t really need if you’re trying to do it as an offering to God.
  2. Increase the quality. No one wants to offer something up to God and say, “Well, it’s good enough.”
  3. Increase work ethic. It feels good to work as hard as you can for your God. I love turning time and talent into an offering for God whether it’s a sermon, a lesson plan, a family vacation, or a hard morning workout. Working in God's presence as an offering to God changes everything.

Brothers and sisters, remember what happens in our worship service each week. We are cut up by the word of God, consecrated for his service. We are set apart again for his service. We eat with him as royal sons and daughters at his table. And then we are sent out. We are commissioned into the world. We ascend the mountain of God to meet with him, and then he sends us back to earth so that we can keep taking dominion, keep praying and laboring for his will to be done on earth as it is in heaven. So keep building as an offering to God.

3. Do your assigned building.

There’s two things I want you to notice here. First, no one was a professional mason. The goldsmiths and perfumers probably didn't think that this was going to be what they were remembered for. It was their amateur work of wall building that we remember them for, not the crowns or signet rings. Similarly, we should put our back into whatever work God puts in front of us, whether or not it’s “our thing.” We can’t see God’s story yet. Who knows what “amateur work” God will put in front of us that will turn out to be the most important thing that we do.

Second, each man was assigned a specific section. Brothers and sisters, put your name on something. In fifty years, let's say our kids are sitting around at a potluck after service, recounting the stories of God's faithfulness and how much he has done. What will they say you did? What will your name be on?

4. Be scrappy.

Archeological evidence shows us that this wall was nothing impressive. It was the work of amateurs doing their best with sub-par materials. That’s not a bad thing. Don’t fall prey to the thinking, “If only I had more money, or a different location, or more time, or more anything else, then I could really make something great” Remember Caleb’s message from November 2. You have everything you need. So get to work and be scrappy.

Focus on progress, not being perfect.

5. Build humbly.

Be under the authorities that God has placed you under to build. The leaders of Tekoa, according to 3:5, “would not stoop to serve their Lord.” In the ESV you’ll see a footnote saying that you could also translate it “lords.” I think that makes better sense in this context. The Tekoaite rulers wouldn’t take a supervisor. They wouldn’t accept the authority that God placed them under for the building.

You may not be under the authority that you want to be. But it’s where God has placed you to build, so build from there! If you’re able to get out and serve with more freedom, do so. But until then, do everything that you can.

6. Let forgiveness be your fuel.

Finally, there are three men listed here whose participation is really important. They are not mentioned with special fanfare. But their work here is significant. Meremoth, Meshullum, and Malchijah were listed at the end of Ezra as men who intermarried with foreign wives.

There are many of us who have made really big mistakes. Mistakes that we think we shouldn’t be able to come back from. But hear me loud and clear: the mercy of Christ has never met a sin so big it couldn’t be washed away. Confess your sin to God and believe he has forgiven you. God does not expect you to spend the rest of your life groveling before him. He wants you to believe him when he says you are forgiven and that he loves you.

So if you’d messed up, even really bad, see the example of Malkijah. Don’t stop building. Confess your sin, receive forgiveness, and get back to work. You aren’t saved because you’re the kind of person who doesn’t make mistakes. You’re saved because God loved you.

2. Build as an offering.

3. Do your assigned building.

4. Be scrappy.

5. Build humbly.

6. Let forgiveness be your fuel.

Introduction

Last week I was in Washington DC for a church planting conference. While I was there I visited the new National World War I Memorial. The function of a war memorial is to help us remember the sacrifice of the soldiers, but also to inspire us to action. And that memorial did excellently. It was sobering and inspiring. The figures of the men fighting capture the desperation and their belief in the cause. On the back of the monument is an excerpt from a poem by Archibald MacLeish, a WWI veteran.

"Whether our lives and our deaths were for peace and a new hope, or for nothing, we cannot say. It is you who must say this. They say: we leave you our deaths. Give them their meaning."

The monument is meant to inspire those who visit to make, by their living, the sacrifice of those who died worthwhile. To create the civilization of peace and hope they fought for a reality. That's the effect this list of names from Nehemiah has on me. This list of names comes as a kind of grand accomplishment. It's reminiscent of the mighty-men list from Samuel. It recounts their great struggle to do what God gave them to do despite the hardship and danger. They spurned discouragement and pain and with God's help accomplished something magnificent.

My aim today is that this list of names would have that effect on you. I want the purpose Nehemiah included this list for to have its effect in your soul. I want you to leave praying for God’s help to put your name on something.

So let’s jump in by first understanding the literary purpose of this list in Nehemiah 3. We’re going to spend most of the sermon here, understanding and applying the overall purpose of this list. Then at the end we will come to some specific lessons we should learn from it.

What is the literary purpose of this list? To show the importance of their work.

When we get a list of names like this, the most important question to ask is what it's doing here. For instance, think about the genealogy at the beginning of Matthew. What's most important isn't the particular names (although there’s some really interesting stuff there for those who dig). What's most important is the function of genealogy. In Matthew's case, it's to show that Jesus is the king of Judah, the blessing from Abraham, and the second Adam.

So what's the point of this list? The best way to find out is to see if there are any other lists structured like this one in the Bible. Notice how this list in Nehemiah 3 is structured.

”The sons of Hassenaah build the Fish Gate. They laid its beams and set its doors, its bolts, and its bars. And next to them Meremoth of the son of Uriah, son Hakka repaired. And next to them Meshullam the son of Berechiah, son of Meshezabel repaired…”

It’s got a regular pattern: name, provenance, accomplishment and relevant comments. There’s another list structured just like this in 2 Samuel 23. There the mighty men of David are listed by name and with their accomplishments.

“Jeshebbassebeth a Tehchemonite; he was chief of the three. He wielded his spear against eight hundred whom he killed at one time…next to him was Shammah, the son of Agee the Hararite. The Philistines gathered together at Lehi, where there was a plot of ground full of lentils, and the men fled from the Philistines. But he took his stand in the midst of the plot and defended it and struck down the Philistines, and the LORD worked a great victory.”

Do you see how similar they are? They even use the same connecting words, “and next to him.” This list is presenting the men in Nehemiah as a new set of mighty men who waged war in wall-building. The purpose of including it is to aggrandize, to exalt the work and those who labored in it. To place it in scripture alongside David’s mighty men.

Why does Nehemiah think this rebuilding is such a big deal?

But that leaves me with another question: why do they think their work is so grand? Why do they think they are like new mighty men? I mean, the wall was important and everything, but these people came from Susa. Compare the walls of Susa to these walls and it's no contest. These just aren't that impressive. We know from archeological evidence that the wall was really just thrown together with whatever stones they could find and from the rubble left after the destruction. It wasn't the work of professional masons. It was amateur work. So why did they think it was so impressive? The place to look to find out is Ezekiel.

While Israel was in exile, Ezekiel wrote his great vision of a temple and a city. Fresh on their mind as they returned was Ezekiel 47. In that chapter the prophet sees water flowing out from the temple, through the courtyard and the city and out the city gates to the world. As the water continues out from the city it grows deeper and deeper. Around the river stand trees with good fruit. And the angel says (and just think about this symbolism for a moment),

"On the banks, on both sides of the river, there will grow all kinds of trees for food. Their leaves will not wither, nor their fruit fail, but they will bear fresh fruit every month, because the water for them flows from the sanctuary. Their fruit will be for food and their leaves for healing."

You don't need to be an Bible expert to understand the symbolism here. Water flows from the temple, through the city to the world. God provides fresh water, good fruit from trees, and healing in the leaves of the trees. This is a vision of God saving grace coming to the world. And the vision labors to show where it comes from. The waters don’t fall out of the sky. They come from the temple and through Jerusalem. They travel through the city, and then to the world. He graciously provides the nations with trees of life for sustenance and healing through the city which represents his chosen people.

Their work is so grand because Ezekiel 47 presents it as the first step in the healing of the world.

From where Nehemiah sits, rebuilding this city is the first step in the healing of the world. He doesn’t expect an actual river to flow out of the temple. He understands that’s the grace and saving power of God. But there does need to be a civilization, a city on a hill, a people of God for that power to flow out of according to the vision. God’s grace isn’t pictured as just dropping out of heaven on the world. It’s coming from the capital of his people.

So why is building the temple and city such a grand accomplishment in Nehemiah’s estimation as he writes? Why does he present the workers as new mighty men? Not because in itself it's so impressive but because it contributes to God's mission of redemption.

I have mentioned to you before the story of JFK visiting NASA. While he was there, he met a janitor and asked him, “So what is it you do here?” And the janitor said, “I’m putting a man on the moon.” He understood how his little piece fit into the grand mission, and so, his little role was filled with grand dignity. That’s what’s happening with this list. You could think about it like the training montage leading up to the big fight in a boxing movie. The point isn't to emphasize the type of training, or exactly how many practice jabs are thrown. The point is to emphasize how important the upcoming fight is.

In the case of Nehemiah, the specific outcome of their work isn’t all that stupendous. The outcome doesn’t make sense of why it looks so big. So we ask, “Why was it so big to them? “ Because of the great mission they are on: to build the civilization, the society, that God will use to send his living, healing water to the ends of the earth.

How does Ezekiel 47 and the rebuilding efforts in Nehemiah 3 connect to our efforts here in the New Covenant?

So we can understand why they think their rebuilding efforts are such a big deal. But how does that connect to us?

Well, it turns out that in God’s plan the river of life wasn’t going to flow from Jerusalem at that time. Rather than acting like a city on a hill, we’ll find by the end of Nehemiah that the people are acting just like the nations. Instead of being the conduit for God’s redeeming grace to flow to the nations, they’re letting the wicked sewage for he nations back up and compromise their city.

But a few hundred years later the son of God came to Jerusalem. He taught us the words of life that become in us a well of living water, died as a propitiation for the whole world (not just Israel), poured out his Spirit into His people, and then sent them out from Jerusalem, to Samaria, to the ends of the earth.

Let me trace for you more closely how the Nehemiah 3 rebuilding in pursuit of the Ezekiel 47 vision connects to us.

God gives us not one, but two great prophetic images of the world being renewed by his grace where that grace flows out from a city—the symbol of civilization. We’ve already discussed Ezekiel’s vision where water flows from the temple through the city to the ends of the earth. But that vision is picked up again in Revelation. There we can receive a similar calling as Nehemiah and his mighty men did.

In Revelation the New Jerusalem comes down out of heaven. It’s presented as the height of human civilization. It’s decked with rubies and jasper and emeralds. But in this city there is no temple. “For the temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb.” The city has no temple because God dwells in the whole city with its inhabitants. So where is the water going to flow from if there’s no temple?

In the following verses, Ezekiel’s vision is more or less repeated. The nations walk by the light of the city. The kings of the earth bring their glory and honor into it. The river of life flows out from the throne of God through the city to the world with trees bearing fruit and whose leaves are for the healing of the nations. Remember, this city, the New Jerusalem is the bride of the Lamb. It’s us, the church.

In John 7 Jesus says that rivers of living water will flow from him for the world. Then John clarifies that this means the Holy Spirit. But now the Holy Spirit has come and is us. We are the city that the living water comes to the world through.

Christ’s church is the city through which God’s power and grace flows to the nations so that their kings bring their glory to God.

This isn’t a way to sneak post-millenialism in for everyone. As far as I know, I’m the only one of our four pastors who is post-mil. You can believe God’s grace through the New Jerusalem heals the world gradually now (post-mil), cataclysmically in a future millennium (pre-mil), or generally on the new earth (a-mil). But no matter what, we are the society, the city that brings God’s blessings to the world for their healing.

Jesus died so that we, the church, would become a new humanity. Rather than being separated by national boundaries, like Israel, we are set apart by the sacraments: baptism and the Lord’s Supper. We are those washed clean by his grace. We are those who eat with God. Set free from the Old Adam, we get to make a new way of life based on the life, death, and accomplishment of the New Adam.

That’s the connection between Nehemiah 3 building Jerusalem and us building the New Jerusalem. We are a New Jerusalem.

So what exactly are we building?

So we are the New Jerusalem, the bride of Christ, the church.. 1 Peter 2 describes us as God’s new spiritual house that God is building.

“As you come to him, a living stone rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen and precious, you yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house…for it stands in scripture: ‘Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone, a cornerstone chosen and precious, and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame.’”

God is building us up. We are living stones added to his great structure when we believe in Christ, the cornerstone of the whole thing.

But not only are we being built, God calls us to participate in the building. Ephesians 4 tells us that Jesus gave the church apostles, prophets, avengelists, and pastors to equip the saints for the work of the ministry and then defines that work of ministry as “building up the body of Christ.” He tells us later, “Speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ, from whom he whole body, joined and held together by every joint with which it is equipped, when each part is working properly, makes the body grow so that it builds itself up in love.”

So we are both being built by God (1 Peter 2) and we are called to participate in building up the body of Christ (Ephesians 4).

But what exactly are we being and building? Scripture gives us many metaphors to explain what the church is to us. It says we are a body that should grow to maturity, a city that should grow to be like the New Jerusalem, the bride of Christ that must one day be perfect and spotless, and even more. But you’ll notice that in all of the metaphors used to describe the church, there is maturing and perfecting to take place. God has an end goal in mind for ua—and that’s what we should build toward. I believe that end goal is Christian civilization.

What is “civilization?”

First let me define what I mean by Christian civilization. Then I’ll show you why that’s the blueprint we should build towards.

Civilization is the high level of technology and culture that comes as a result of humans working together. Civilization emerges from generations of men and women working together to make a way of life. As such, a civilization is the manifestation of a culture. If you want to know what a culture is like, look at their cities. In cities, their values are cast in steel and stone. And, of course, we know that culture flows from worship.

So, people live together and worship God. They establish a God-honoring culture that finds specific ways to demonstrate joy and love and grace and celebrate the gifts of God. As time goes on, they will advance in technology and their culture will become thick enough that you’d look at their way of life and call it a “civilization.”

Civilization is the organized way of life, including technology, architecture, and customs, created by a culture advancing to a sufficient degree.

Now, what makes a civilization Christian? A civilization is Christian if it embodies Christ's values. A Christian civilization isn’t necessarily all Christian. That is, not everyone in a Christian city or Christian nation needs to be Christians to call it “Christian.” That won’t be possible until we live on the new earth. But conscious obedience to Christ as a people constitutes a Christian people.

So that’s what I mean by a Christian civilization. Christians working together over generations to produce a distinctly God-honoring culture and for that culture to overflow in technology, customs, architecture, and all the things that make up a full way of life that consciously obeys Christ.

How is “Christian civilization” different from the “Kingdom of God” and “Christendom?”

Let me differentiate this term “Christian civilization” from a couple other related terms: the Kingdom of God and Christendom.

The Kingdom of God is the rule and realm of God. Kings have both rule and realm; power and a place. In one sense, God’s kingdom includes everything, since he has all power and authority and dominion. But in another sense, the kingdom is coming and growing, just like Scripture says. Jesus’ parables do not describe the kingdom as the static, always infinite power of God. Instead, it grows and works through the world. Think of the parables where Jesus says the kingdom is like leaven working through a loaf. So, God’s kingdom is in one sense all that there is and in another sense, the places on earth where his will is done as it is in heaven.

The Kingdom of God is not the same as Christian Civilization. Christian Civilizations are manifestations of the kingdom of God because they are places where God’s will is done on earth. But the Kingdom of God is a much bigger category that in one sense includes all things and in another sense includes all Christian civilizations and anyplace, even if it’s just a household, where God’s will is being done faithfully.

Christendom is another overlapping term. The term “Christendom” is often used in two senses. First, it means the global church—all believers still alive on the earth. Someone might say “Christendom is growing” and they mean that the global church is growing. A second sense of “Christendom” is used by Wilson in his book Mere Christendom. There he advocates for a civilization that is given to Jesus in every sphere, especially politically. There's a lot of overlap in how Wilson uses the term Christendom and how I'm using the term "Christian civilization" in this sermon. However, "Christendom" often focuses on the political ramifications of having Christ as lord of a country (like including the Apostle’s Creed in the constitution, or Sabbath laws). I'm all about those ramifications applied in wisdom. But as I talk about Christian Civilization, I'm talking about way more than politics.

Christian civilization is the fruit of generations and generations of Christian obedience. It takes thousands and thousands of faithful moms and dads. Tens of thousands of kids honoring their parents. It takes thousands of moms who create the culture around holidays by making sure there’s gingerbread men to be dentally decapitated around Christmas, and ham to eat over Easter, and the “Happy Birthday” sign is hung in celebration of God’s image born into a home. It takes thousands of men committed to serving their community at great cost to themselves. Men who pour out their lives to roof houses, run electrical wiring, manage employees, found organizations, and serve the body politic.

Over time, people's intelligence increases as they become better at educating their children. Their morality increases as they learn to parent more faithfully. They grow in knowledge of the Bible as their pastors minister more effectively. They develop helpful patterns of celebration and thanksgiving around holidays. They establish normal household rhythms that most follow. They create new technologies that buy back more time to turn a profit on.

Christian civilization is what happens when all of life is taken captive to Christ for generations and God blesses their efforts. Now let me give you two Biblical reasons Christian civilization is what we should aim for:

Christian civilization is the result of taking dominion.

From the moment God created humanity, he expected us to take dominion by building God-honoring civilizations. It was true of humanity in Adam. It’s true of the new humanity in Christ too.

God designed humans to create civilizations. He made us so that civilization is inevitable. You can create cities and technologies that incarnate a God-honoring culture or that incarnate our wicked hatred for God. But there’s no way to avoid civilization. God is a community in himself, three in one as the Trinity. He made us to live together in community to demonstrate what he is like.

Civilization is so natural to how God made us that even in our fallen state, it took only zero generations to plant fields and cultivate livestock and only took seven generations to go from skin garments to tents, livestock, musical instruments, bronze and even iron tools. Think about that! Adam was still alive when someone made the first iron plow and strummed the first lyre. God didn’t create the world hoping that we wouldn't inhabit it. He made it for us to tame, to take dominion over. God designed humans to civilize earth. That’s part of why the Genesis story tells us about the raw materials, like gold and other precious metals and rivers all around Eden. God set them in a perfect place to begin a God-honoring civilization.

God did not make us "noble savages" who are better on our own. That was Rousseau's idea in the Enlightenment era. And it was beaten into the American consciousness by the transcendentalist writers who wrote as though if we just walked away from civilization and joined them out at Walden pond everything would be better. Instead of believing the biblical doctrine that we are born sinful and that civilization often restrains our wickedness, they believed that we were good or at least neutral and that it is civilization that made us wicked. But they are wrong. Human culture and civilization is good. We don’t mess up everything we touch. Earth would not be better off without us. God put us here to complete and glorify earth by working together. God prepared the earth for us to civilize, even providing the raw materials for us to use. He put gold in the earth for us to dig up. He pressed diamonds for us to learn to work them. He stashed oceans of natural gas for us to harness.

But Rousseau's ideas are still alive and well in today's conservative movement. It’s the underlying assumption behind the conservative tendency to retreat and get away and try to be alone. “That civilization will infect me and my family. If we can just get away then we’ll be safe.” Well, civilization will infect you. It may pull your family down. But getting away isn’t the solution. You’ll find plenty of sin in your own hearts even if you retreat. The solution God has provided for us in Christ is a new society, a new civilization: the church of God that builds one another up.

We want to retreat from the civilization that secularism has made because in it we are like fish out of water. But that doesn’t mean civilization is bad. Our cities encapsulate our values as a people. In Lord of the Rings, the Elven cities are works of art. They harmoniously live in nature while making a comfortable civilization. The dwarves make another kind of civilization under the mountains that is wholly different but still grand and impressive. Of course secularism makes a sad civilization. We can either live in it begrudgingly or we can build our own. But there’s no opting out of contributing to a civilization. The only question is whether or not it will honor God. The question we should ask is, “What kind of civilization will we build? How will we capture our love and joy and peace? How will we put hospitality in brick and mortar?”

To sum up this first reason, God created humans to take dominion by forming civilization. That’s not just true of humanity in Adam. It’s true of the new humanity in Christ too.

Christian civilization is the result of “teaching them all I have commanded you.”

God doubled down on this plan when we formed the church. If you gather Christians and they live faithfully together, they will form a distinct culture and eventually their own civilization living within the nations of the world. I believe this is exactly what Jesus has in mind when he says that the Kingdom of God is like leaven working through a loaf or a mustard seed that will grow. Traditions form, culture builds, holidays are established, integrity is enshrined in law, and before long, you have a Christian civilization forming from within the nations of the world. If God is gracious, they are transformed from the inside out.

Perhaps you haven’t realized how much of what you think of as the “secular” blessings of living in America are really Christ’s blessings.

  • When you drive home today, you expect everyone to obey the rules of the road and stop at red lights, not pass you illegally or recklessly, and generally obey the speed limit. Those of us who have travelled outside of the West, where Christianity has had a long and profound impact know that areas where Christianity hasn’t been so dominant, the traffic laws are viewed as suggestions. Our civilization enshrined love for neighbor and integrity to the law in our traffic values.
  • In our country, contract law is still actually enforced. When you sign your name to something, you have to really mean it. But if you go outside of the west, you’ll find that many governments don’t enforce contract law. We’ve institutionalized letting our yes be yes and our no be no and have reaped the benefits of it for centuries.

More recently our civilization has abandoned Christ in areas that we used to follow him. For instance, allowing no-fault divorce, allowing men to complete in women’s sports, promoting critical theory in schools and universities, subsidizing sloth and single-parenthood with the welfare system. Has any of this disobedience to Christ given us good fruit? Of course not.

That’s the difference between Christian civilization and wicked civilization. This is just a way of saying that when we obey Christ in all of life, all of life will be transformed. As it all comes under his lordship, Jesus doesn’t destroy the idea of human civilization and send us to live in our own separate monasteries. He creates a new civilization. When he sent us out into he world, he didn’t mean that we’d just evangelize the nations and then leave their laws, customs, patterns, and habits alone. He told us to teach them everything he commanded us. Our job is to disciple people so that they obey Christ in all of life. And when we do that to more than a few people, the inevitable result is Christian civilization.

Learn from these mighty men to rebuild Christian civilization.

So, we’ve spent most of our time understanding the literary purpose for this list of names (to present a new list of mighty men), why they viewed their work as so grand and important (they believed it was the first step for the healing of the world according to Ezekiel 47), and then seeing how Ezekiel 47 and their building connects to us in the New Covenant. I’ve argued that the connection means that we should build Christian civilizations.

Now, here are six lessons for us to learn about building Christian civilization that we can learn from these mighty men listed in Nehemiah 3.

1. Build to win.

This whole list comes as the response to the wicked rulers of the land. The last thing Nehemiah told their enemies is that God’s people will build the city, God will make them prosper, and the wicked will have no portion in it. They are in a competition, a near war with the enemies of God and building and fighting to win. So they roll up their sleeves. Imagine this scene as a movie. Nehemiah tells them “We will rebuild this city! And you will have no right or claim here!” This list is the building montage. They get to work trying to win.

Don't box as one beating the air. You've got work to do. This is why godly ambition is so important. Your problem is probably not that you are too ambitious, but that you aren't ambitious enough. There is territory to be gained, markets to win, real-estate to own, and talented employees to attract. And in all those endeavors, as everywhere, God makes all the difference.

So build with the intent to beat your enemies. Build better companies, more hospitable homes, more just cities, more refreshed friends, more capable children, more beautiful music—an overall more attractive culture.

2. Build as an offering.

Look at it in 3:1. The first one to begin the building is the high priest. When he completed his work, they consecrated it as a kind of first-fruits offering. When the wall is complete, they will dedicate it to God as well. That’s significant. A wall isn’t a holy space. They were doing “secular” work (wall building) but they dedicated it to God. This is how we need to learn to think about everything we do.

In one of Wilson’s books, he comments that if we are living sacrifices, then everywhere we are is an altar. Monday morning, your desk is an altar. Monday night, your dinner table is an altar. Whatever you do, you do as an offering to God.

So as you do your part to build Christian civilization for God, give it to him as an offering. Give him the Thanksgiving dinner, the family gathering, the sour dough, the spreadsheet, the sales pitch, all of it. As you do, it will:

  1. Increase your integrity. You won’t feel right selling someone hard on a product they don’t really need if you’re trying to do it as an offering to God.
  2. Increase the quality. No one wants to offer something up to God and say, “Well, it’s good enough.”
  3. Increase work ethic. Working in God's presence as an offering to God changes everything.

Brothers and sisters, remember what happens in our worship service each week. We are cut up as a sacrifice by the word of God and set apart for his service. Then we eat with him as royal sons and daughters at his table. And then we are sent out. We are commissioned into the world. We ascend the mountain of God to meet with him, and then he sends us back to earth so that we can keep taking dominion.

3. Do your assigned building.

There’s two things I want you to notice here. First, no one was a professional mason. The goldsmiths and perfumers probably didn't think that this was going to be what they were remembered for. It was their amateur work of wall building that we remember them for, not the crowns or signet rings. Similarly, we should put our back into whatever work God puts in front of us, whether or not it’s “our thing.” We can’t see God’s story yet. Who knows what “amateur work” God will put in front of us that will turn out to be the most important thing that we do.

Second, each man completed a specific section. They weren’t all responsible for the whole wall. They were responsible for their portion. Brothers and sisters, find your portion. Put your name on something. In fifty years, let's say our kids are sitting around at a potluck after service, recounting the stories of God's faithfulness and how much he has done. What will they say you did?

4. Be scrappy.

Archeological evidence shows us that this wall was nothing impressive. It was the work of amateurs doing their best with sub-par materials. That’s not a bad thing. Don’t fall prey to the thinking, “If only I had more money, or a different location, or more time, or more anything else, then I could really make something great” Remember Caleb’s message from November 2. You have everything you need. So get to work with what you have.

Focus on progress, not being perfect.

5. Build humbly.

Be under the authorities that God has placed you under to build. The leaders of Tekoa, according to 3:5, “would not stoop to serve their Lord.” In the ESV you’ll see a footnote saying that you could also translate it “lords.” I think that makes better sense in this context. The Tekoaite rulers wouldn’t take a supervisor. They wouldn’t accept the authority that God placed them under for the building.

You may not be under the authority that you want to be. Maybe you’re working for a boss you don’t like. But it’s where God has placed you to build, so build from there! If you’re able to get out and serve with more freedom, do so.

Kids, God placed you under your parents authority. Don’t be like the Tekoaite rulers who couldn’t submit. They are recorded forever in Scripture to their shame as those who couldn’t humbly give honor to where honor was due. Obey God and honor your parents by obeying them. Build with them.

6. Receive forgiveness.

Finally, there are three men listed here whose participation is really important. They are not mentioned with special fanfare. But their work here is significant. Meremoth, Meshullum, and Malchijah were listed at the end of Ezra as men who intermarried with foreign wives.

There are many of us who have made really big mistakes. Mistakes that we think we shouldn’t be able to come back from. But hear me loud and clear: the mercy of Christ has never met a sin so big it couldn’t be washed away. Confess your sin to God and believe he has forgiven you. God does not expect you to spend the rest of your life groveling before him. He wants you to believe him when he says you are forgiven and that he loves you.

So if you’d messed up, even really bad, see the example of Malkijah. Don’t stop building. Confess your sin, receive forgiveness, and get back to work. You aren’t saved because you’re the kind of person who doesn’t make mistakes. You’re not saved because of anything you’ve done. You’ll never serve enough to repay the grace of God. You’re saved because God loved you. Receive his forgiveness and love, and get back to work.

Conclusion
