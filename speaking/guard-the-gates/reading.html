Scripture: Nehemiah 6:15–7:73

Main Point: God helped Israel complete the wall (6:15–16), but Tobiah slips through the gates (6:17–19). Therefore Nehemiah must populate the city with faithful, God-fearing men who won’t compromise (7:1–73).

Introduction

I’m going to give you some options, and I want you to think about which one is easier and which one is harder.

  • Starting a family or raising children.
  • Getting the job or working the job.
  • Starting a business or scaling a business.
  • Starting a diet or staying on a diet.
  • Starting a book or finishing a book.

The point is clear: it is much easier to get something started than it is to keep it going. Anyone who has ever begun an exercise program and then failed to finish it knows this. This is the tipping point that Nehemiah’s project reaches in our passage. The wall is completed. They now have everything they need to live as a distinct, faithful city that honors God. But building the wall was not the hardest part. The hardest part will be building a distinct, faithful city that truly honors God. Nehemiah sets himself to that task by appointing faithful governors over Jerusalem and determining who may settle inside its walls.

Here is the basic story of this passage, and the outline of this sermon.

  1. God helped Israel complete the wall (6:15–16)
  2. but Tobiah slips through the gates (6:17–19)
  3. Therefore Nehemiah must populate the city with faithful, God-fearing men who won’t compromise (7:1–73).

God helped Israel complete the wall (6:15–16)

Nehemiah 6:15 is an incredible testimony to God’s sovereignty over kings, nations, and history. Hundreds of years earlier he had told Moses that this is what would happen. In Deuteronomy 30, God declares that even after the curses fall on Israel, when they call on him he will “restore their fortunes” and gather them again from the places where he had scattered them (Deut. 30:3). Before the exile, the prophet Jeremiah predicted seventy years until the decree of Cyrus that would allow them to return and rebuild the temple and the city—and that is exactly what occurred. In the midst of sabotage, hardship, and constant opposition from their enemies, the wall is completed. They even record the exact date it was finished.

This completion was fixed in the mind of God. Yet it certainly did not feel that way as the story unfolded, did it? It looked as though the project could fail at any moment. There were countless opportunities for God’s enemies to triumph over his people. The adversaries halted the temple building under Zerubbabel, yet it resumed and was finished. Ezra and those returning with him traveled without an army for protection, yet they arrived safely in Jerusalem. Sanballat and Tobiah tried jeering, propaganda, frontal military assault, and sabotage to stop the work. Internally, the people were fracturing as the rich took advantage of the poor. It did not look as though this had to happen. But it was exactly what God had planned. All those trials were the very means he appointed to bring his people to the day when the wall stood complete.

God does not always make the path he calls us to walk easy and smooth. In the midst of trials and hardship we tend to interpret closed doors and difficulties as evidence that we should turn back. This passage suggests otherwise. Notice that Zerubbabel, Ezra, and Nehemiah never treated these attacks or setbacks as signs that God no longer wanted Jerusalem rebuilt. They did not read closed doors as God saying “no,” nor did they assume they should simply wait for an “open door.” That mindset is simply waiting for the path of least resistance and assuming that must be God’s way. If Nehemiah had taken the path of least resistance, he would still be the king’s cupbearer. Instead he is here, and the wall stands completed despite every difficulty.

When we conclude that difficulty means we should turn back because God must not want us to continue, we are assuming that God wants our lives to be easy and comfortable all the time. He loves us too much for that. Some of you have endured truly significant trials; others, less so. But if you look back across your life, you will usually see that it is when the pressure is turned up and the trials are intense that God forges a deeper level of faith in us. Like steel that must be heated until it glows before it can be forged and shaped, God normally turns up the heat to form our hearts.

Our elder brother Jesus is the great dragon-slayer. He did hard things, and he calls us to imitate him. We cannot yet handle the dragon, but he is preparing us. Every trial, hardship, and difficulty is God kindly readying you for what is coming next and shaping you so that you and I become more mature.

Some of you college students have just finished a semester that proved far harder than you expected, and you have considered dropping out. That difficulty is not God telling you to throw in the towel. Children, some of you are taking piano lessons, dance lessons, or learning some other skill. Learning anything new is hard. The difficulty is not a reason to quit. Some of you are facing a serious roadblock in your career. That difficulty is not evidence that you are in the wrong line of work.

I linger on this point because we know beyond doubt that rebuilding the temple and the wall was God’s plan. And yet it was still extraordinarily difficult for them. Therefore our assumption that life should be easy—or at least easier than it is—must be abandoned. Life is not supposed to be easy. It is not supposed to be comfortable. God did not design it that way. He sovereignly decreed that the temple and this wall would be rebuilt, yet he did not make the path smooth and effortless. So let us take God at his word when he says, “Count it all joy, my brothers, when you meet trials of various kinds, for you know that the testing of your faith produces steadfastness” (James 1:2–3).

52 Days

Verse 15 tells us that the wall was completed in fifty-two days. Some believe this summarizes the entire project, meaning that from the day Nehemiah first called them to rebuild in chapter 2 until the walls were finished was only fifty-two days. Archaeological evidence that the walls were built hastily lends some support to this view.

Two considerations, however, incline me away from it.

  1. Chapter 6:1 states that the wall had already been rebuilt up to the point where only the doors and gates remained to be set. It then recounts the three attempts at sabotage. Only after that does 6:15 say the wall was finished in fifty-two days. The flow of the text suggests that the fifty-two days run from the letter sent by Sanballat and Geshem in 6:2 rather than from Nehemiah’s arrival. If the entire project took only fifty-two days, then all of the following events occurred in less than two months:
  2. They begin building (3:1).
  3. Sanballat and Tobiah begin their jeering (4:1).
  4. Sanballat and Tobiah hear that the work is progressing well (4:7).
  5. They attempt to use propaganda to frighten the builders (4:10).
  6. They prepare a surprise attack (4:11).
  7. Israel prepares to defend itself (4:13).
  8. Israel returns to work (4:15).
  9. Nehemiah addresses the oppression of the poor and calls an assembly (5).
  10. Sanballat, Tobiah, and Geshem attempt three different schemes to sabotage Nehemiah (6:1–14).

It is possible all of that happened in fifty-two days, but given the speed of communication in that day, it seems unlikely to me.

  1. Nehemiah left Susa in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes (2:1) and did not return until the thirty-second year (13:6). He spent twelve years away. Most of the rest of the book recounts a single extended season in which the people gather, rehearse God’s faithfulness, confess their sins, make a covenant, settle in Jerusalem, and dedicate the wall. If the wall took only fifty-two days, then Nehemiah spent the bulk of those twelve years doing we do not know what, with a gap that is not clearly marked in the text.

I could spend time revisiting the chronological questions tied to this issue. Instead, we will stay with the thrust of the text itself. Those questions are interesting, but I cannot answer them with certainty, and they are far less edifying than the story the Holy Spirit has given us.

Enemies fall in their own esteem

Verse 16 tells us that when the enemies heard the wall was finished, “they fell greatly in their own esteem, for they perceived that this work had been accomplished with the help of our God.”

First, consider the nations falling greatly in their own esteem. Part of what we see here is a partial fulfillment of Nehemiah’s prayer in 4:4. When Sanballat and Tobiah first jeered at the work, Nehemiah prayed, “Turn back their taunt on their own heads.” In their mockery they had exalted themselves like men standing on a mountaintop—important, strong, powerful leaders of a great nation. Now they see, “We could not stop them. We had one goal and we failed,” and they fall greatly in their own esteem.

This is a marvelous illustration of the power of simply keeping your head down and building. Nothing disheartens God’s enemies more, nothing discourages them more, nothing takes the wind out of their sails like the success of his people. In other words, building is fighting. So keep your head down and build.

Second, notice that the enemies themselves give God credit for the wall. Nehemiah records that they perceived the work had been accomplished with the help of our God. They do not seize credit for their success, even though their labor was the means God used. Nehemiah does not try to steal the glory, thinking, “Who was it in the trenches all those weeks? Who kept the rich from abusing the poor? Who organized every section of the wall? Look at this wall I have built by my talent and ingenuity.” Instead he gladly acknowledges the help God gave them. Not only had Nehemiah defeated Sanballat; God had defeated Sanballat’s gods. That is why God’s help made them fall in their own esteem. They saw that the God of Jacob is stronger than the gods of Sanballat.

Brothers and sisters, we must learn to give God credit for every good effort we make. Think of 1 Corinthians 15:10, where Paul says, “I worked harder than any of them, though it was not I, but the grace of God that is with me.” Or Colossians 1:29: “I toil, struggling with all his energy that he powerfully works within me.” There is no contradiction between working hard and trusting God, because your energy and effort are not finally your own. Your works are God’s grace. Your effort is God’s gift. David Mathis, in his book Habits of Grace, puts it memorably: you receive the grace of effort by doing. But the fact that you did the doing does not make it any less a gift from God.

This is astonishing. It means there is really nothing you can claim as finally your own. Nothing to boast in. Everything you might have boasted in you did with effort that God himself supplied. Even your trying hard is God’s gift. This frees us from any attempt to earn salvation. There is no earning it. You did not run up a great debt of sin that you can repay by doing good works. No. You ran up a great debt of sin, and Jesus paid it in full on the cross. Now every good work you do is more of God’s grace coming your way. You are not getting even with God when you obey; you are going gloriously further into his debt.

So let us put entirely out of our minds any notion of working for salvation. Our good works are God’s works in us. This means God is as sovereignly at work in our sanctification as he was in our justification. In justification he gave us the gift of faith. We did not stir up faith in ourselves; God sovereignly granted it, and we acted on it by repenting and believing. In the same way, when we obey God and pursue sanctification through strenuous effort, that is God sovereignly sanctifying us, and we get to act it out by giving our full effort. If you want to experience more of the grace of God in sanctification, trust him—and then work even harder. That effort is given to you by God.

So go work harder than the rest, endure, and press through the difficulties God appoints for your steadfastness and stamina. And all the while remember that you struggle with the energy that God supplies: “Not I, but Christ in me.”

The goal is a distinct nation

As I studied this passage, one thing that struck me is how little space is given to the completion of the wall. One verse, then the effect of the wall, and then the narrative moves on to something else. Nehemiah set out to rebuild the wall, so why does its completion receive so little attention? One reason is that the wall was never all he was after. He was seeking to build a distinct Israelite nation—a people in the world but not of the world, a people who would worship God according to his commandments and whose worship would flow from Mount Zion across the whole land.

Chapter 7:4 tells us that “the city was wide and large, but the people within it were few, and no houses had been rebuilt.” That is what Nehemiah will spend the rest of the book seeking to remedy. He must persuade more people to come live in Jerusalem and continue rebuilding so that they have a real city, not merely a military outpost with a temple. If it remains as it is, it will be easy to overrun. It has been safe while they rebuilt because many men were present who would not normally have been there. But when they return to their villages, the city will be vulnerable. The walls are only part of the goal. To have a functioning capital from which a nation can grow again, it needs a population.

What we will see in the rest of chapter 6 is the major obstacle to a healthy capital: Tobiah. Then in chapter 7 and beyond we will see Nehemiah’s strategy. He entrusts what has been completed to faithful, God-fearing men so that he can focus on gathering a faithful, God-fearing population for the city.

So let us begin by looking at Tobiah.

Tobiah slips through the gates (6:17–19)

As the wall is finished, a snake slips in through the gates. Tobiah gains influence among the Israelites because he has married into the right families and his son has done the same. In other words, he plays politics skillfully and positions himself to contend for power alongside Nehemiah. The result is that the nobles of Judah make peace with Tobiah. They send him letters. And they seem to understand the tension between Nehemiah and Tobiah, because they repeatedly speak of all Tobiah’s good deeds in Nehemiah’s presence.

At this point Tobiah is closer to sabotaging the entire project than he has ever been. He is “in” with the Jewish nobles. He now has their ear to persuade them that they need not be as “isolationist” or distinct as Nehemiah is urging. He has the attention of many powerful men—so many that Nehemiah cannot simply expel him or them.

In our own day there are hundreds of Tobiahs who are “in” with the people of God. They have become so embedded that it now sounds intolerant and unkind to insist that we must be a separate and distinct people rather than blend in with everyone else. They have made cultural sensitivity more important than having a distinct culture. They have demanded that Christians abandon their culture and assimilate for the sake of the gospel instead of cultivating and raising our children in a distinctly Christian culture that invites unbelievers in.

It would be easy to say that Tobiah is the problem. He is the one tempting everyone to compromise. But that would be mistaken. Tobiah is certainly the villain, but he is not the main problem. The problem is that the Jewish nobles listen to him. Imagine an army captain whose soldiers begin defecting to the enemy. The main problem is not the enemy’s propaganda; it is the disloyalty of the troops. That is what is happening here. Tobiah is only dangerous because the Jewish nobles are willing to correspond with him and give him a place in their hearts instead of rejecting him as the enemy he is.

What I want us to learn is that our greatest problems are not outside the gates. That would be more comfortable to believe, but it is not true. We serve a God far too powerful for us to hold that illusion. The battle of Jericho should have cured us of the idea that our problem is our external enemy. In that battle the people had no city, no army, and no battle plan. God fought for them, and Jericho fell. There is no enemy that can stand against God. Therefore our greatest problem is never the enemies outside the gates. Our problem is that we let them slip through the gates, write them letters, and sit down to dinner with them. In other words, our problem is our own sinfully compromising hearts.

When we believe our greatest problem is “out there,” we are abdicating responsibility. Christians have too often absorbed the idea (following Rousseau) that the problem is “the system.” Instead of pointing at heterosexual white Christian males as liberals do, we point at rainbow-flag-waving secularists. But we are doing the same thing as the homeless man who blames “the system” for his unemployment or the activist who blames “the hegemony” for society’s ills. When we point outside ourselves first, we are saying, “The problem is out there. It is certainly not mostly me.”

Of course, it is unmanly and pathetic to abdicate responsibility in this way. But it is also an astonishing affront to God. This is precisely what Adam did in the garden: “The woman you gave me.” We say, “It is the world you put me in, God. It is the system you placed around me.” When we point the finger at Tobiah we are ultimately blaming God instead of repenting of our own compromising hearts.

Let me give you some examples of places you may be trying to shift responsibility and blame “the system” instead of repenting of your own sin:

  • A young man who says the problem with his lust is the prevalence of pornography rather than his own perverse desire.
  • Parents who say their children’s misbehavior is the result of a label some psychiatrist gave them rather than their own failure to discipline.
  • A family in financial trouble that blames “the economy” rather than their unwise spending and lack of planning.
  • A college student who blames bad grades on the addictiveness of social media rather than their own lack of self-control.
  • A father who blames his angry outburst on a hard day at work rather than his own anger.
  • A woman who blames her nagging on her husband or children rather than her own discontentment.

What we are really saying in all of this is, “But for this circumstance, I would not have sinned. Therefore the problem is mostly the circumstance or the person who caused it.” That gets the biblical order exactly backward. I appreciate Pastor Dustin’s illustration of the tea bag. When life is calm, the tea bag sits in cold water and little comes out. When the heat is turned up, what is inside the bag comes out. Blaming “the system” or circumstances for our sin is like blaming the hot water for what was already in the tea bag.

The world will always provide ample opportunity for temptation. The main problem is that we are lured and enticed by our own desire. That is our problem. We have a mindset ready to make peace with sin and with the world rather than make war against it and stay watchful.

The simple solution for the nobles was to fear God more than they feared Tobiah and the influential families he had married into. If they had, Tobiah would not have been a problem. But because they did not fear God, they were willing to compromise.

Therefore Nehemiah must populate the city with faithful, God-fearing men who won’t compromise (7:1–73)

Nehemiah sees the danger developing and knows he must appoint the right men to lead in his place so he can turn his attention to populating the city. So he places Jerusalem in the hands of his brother Hanani and Hananiah the commander of the citadel.

Notice the character qualifications Nehemiah requires. He is not merely looking for a talented administrator. He is looking for faithful and God-fearing men, because he knows how desperately those qualities will be needed in the face of Tobiah’s sabotage. Many of the Jewish nobles are neither faithful nor God-fearing—and they are carrying on extensive correspondence with Tobiah.

I want to be the kind of man Nehemiah would trust with the city. So what is he looking for? Faithfulness and the fear of God. What do those look like?

  • Faithfulness is being like a rock—constant, responsible, dependable, trustworthy. It is the kind of person who, when you ask them to do something and they say they will, you never need to follow up. You know it will be done.
  • God-fearing men look to do things God’s way. They see themselves as God’s servants. They are not building their own kingdoms, and therefore they will not compromise or negotiate even when it might increase their power. They cannot be bribed or blackmailed because they fear God more than anything any man can do to them.

If these men were not faithful, they would likely ruin the city by dropping the ball. If they were not God-fearing, they would likely ruin the city by giving in to Tobiah. Both qualities are essential.

Aspire to be like these men. Be so faithful that people trust you with anything, great or small. Once it is in your hands, make it flourish. Begin with yourself—your heart, your body—then your household, your work, and outward from there. Responsibility flows to faithful people. And be so God-fearing that you are not swayed by whose father-in-law someone is. Do not be impressed by the supposed importance of those who compromise. Entrust yourself to God and simply keep doing good.

Appointing the right leaders is vital because a city can have all the right structures—in this case, safe walls—and still fail to be faithful. A city is only as good as its people and its leaders. After you build something, the real work has only just begun. As I said at the beginning, starting is rarely the hardest part. Being faithful for a year or two is easy compared to decades of ordinary, vigilant, disciplined life that produces the fruit we long for. This is true in every sphere.

  • It is much easier to get up early and work hard for a few months than to do so for decades.
  • It is much easier to start a family than to raise faithful, fruitful children.
  • It is much easier to launch a business than to scale it.
  • It is much easier to found a nation than to keep it. (Hence Benjamin Franklin’s well-known remark: “A republic, if you can keep it.”)

The same is true for our church. The last year and a half of beginning will be easy compared to decades of faithful living. And this passage gives us two character qualities to guide us:

  • Faithfulness: being dependable, trustworthy, constant, responsible people.
  • God-fearing: regarding ourselves as owned by God and fearing his disapproval more than any man’s.

Nehemiah found two good men to lead the city. But two men do not make a population. So he turns his attention to assembling a genealogy of everyone who had returned from exile. “Why is that helpful?” you might ask. It gives Nehemiah the list of those who could live in Jerusalem. Once that list is in hand, chapter 8 shows him gathering the people, Ezra reading the law, the people celebrating the Feast of Booths, confessing their sins, consecrating themselves to God, and making a covenant. Only then do they assign who will live in Jerusalem and who will live in the surrounding villages. That is the larger movement of the book.

Look with me at 7:5. What does “God put into my heart” mean? Nehemiah used the same expression in 2:12 when he said God had put it into his heart to rebuild the walls. The Bible uses similar language in a few other places.

  • Exodus 35:34 speaks of Bezalel and the building of the tabernacle.
  • Ecclesiastes 3:11 says God has put eternity into man’s heart.
  • Ezra 7:27 says God put it into the heart of the king to glorify the house of the Lord.
  • 2 Chronicles 9:23 says God put wisdom into the mind of Solomon.

Notice that none of these are momentary impressions. Many today describe strong, fleeting feelings that they must speak to someone about the gospel or they will be disobeying God, and they attribute those feelings to the Holy Spirit. That is not what “God put it into my heart” means in these texts. In every case the thing put into the heart is long-lasting. It does not disappear after a month. It sticks. That is what Nehemiah is saying God did with his desire to reestablish Israel in the land. God gave it to him, and he pursued it for more than twelve years.

So do not take this phrase “God put it into my heart” as support for attributing every momentary impression to God. If God puts something in your heart, it will not simply fall back out. Indeed, in Jeremiah 31 God uses this same language to describe regeneration when he puts his law within our hearts.

How does this list compare to Ezra 2?

Now let us take a moment to consider the list in Nehemiah 7. To us these lists can feel tedious (except perhaps for the challenge of pronouncing all the names). But they mattered greatly to the original audience. In 7:64 we learn that some men were barred from priestly service because their genealogies could not confirm their ancestry. Ezra–Nehemiah contains several such lists.

This list in Nehemiah 7 records those who returned at the very beginning. It should therefore closely match the list in Ezra 2. The two are very similar, yet they differ in some names and numbers. A few people appear in one but not the other; some totals for people and gifts also vary. How do we account for these differences?

The standard scholarly answer attributes them all to copyist errors and similar names. I find this unsatisfying. It implies that while the text may once have been inerrant, it is no longer trustworthy in details such as names. That may seem minor to us, but names were vitally important to those who copied the text. After all, they excluded men from priestly service on the basis of these lists. Moreover, these apparently discrepant lists were transmitted unchanged for a very long time. That is strange if they contained errors, and it should instead remind us how careful the scribes were.

It is more likely that the two genealogies were compiled at different times. A list was made when Ezra arrived. It was later updated. Nehemiah includes the updated version so that we have the benefit of both Ezra 2 and Nehemiah 7.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this passage shows us three things we must carry with us.

  • They completed the wall in spite of great hardship. Do not let hardship look like a warning from God. He never promised you a life without difficulty.
  • Completing the wall did not complete the project, because the real project was to establish a distinct, faithful city. Tobiah worked against that goal. He tempted the Jewish nobles to compromise, and they did because they feared Tobiah more than they feared God. Remember not to imagine that your greatest problem lies anywhere but under your own left collarbone. Do not blame “the system” and thereby blame God. Turn from the sin that remains in your heart and pray that God would purify you.
  • Nehemiah appointed rulers who were faithful and God-fearing, then set in motion a plan to fill Jerusalem with others like them. We will see that plan culminate in the covenant they make in chapter 10. Be like the men he appointed. Be faithful—men and women who are completely trustworthy and true to your word. And be God-fearing—serving him with all your heart, soul, mind, and strength.

Amen.
