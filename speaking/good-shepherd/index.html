A Good Shepherd Lays Down His Life for the Sheep

Nehemiah 5

Preached by Nathan Colestock — February 15, 2026

Christ the King Church · Stillwater, MN

2026-02-15

5:1–5 — Nehemiah Hears That the Poor Are Oppressed

Nehemiah’s entire time as governor of this region has been filled with conflict and crisis. Chapter 5 brings yet another crisis. This one, however, is different, because Nehemiah himself bears some responsibility for it.

How Did the Poor Get into This Situation?

Remember that while the people were working to rebuild the wall in Nehemiah 4, Nehemiah commanded everyone to stay in Jerusalem night and day in order to guard the work from being destroyed by Sanballat and Tobiah. We see this command in 4:22:

Let every man and his servant pass the night within Jerusalem, that they may be a guard for us by night, and may labor by day.

This kept the work safe, but it prevented the Israelites from planting and tending their crops. That is almost certainly why a famine grips the people as chapter 5 opens. A famine is the last thing an agrarian society needs while it is being attacked by enemies and trying to rebuild its capital city. With less food available, prices rise according to the law of supply and demand. The poor and working class cannot afford to eat, and they stand to lose everything. As it relates to the wall, families cannot spare workers to labor on the project if their loved ones have nothing to eat. The work will stall, the people will suffer, and a governor who presides over starvation will not remain governor for long.

A famine presents a great opportunity for the rich to share their wealth and food so that the whole community can survive together. Instead, the rich take advantage of the poor. Look with me at verses 1–5. How exactly are the poor being oppressed? As the famine deepens, three groups emerge: (1) those who can still buy grain for themselves (v. 2), (2) those who must mortgage their fields to buy grain (v. 3), and (3) those who have already mortgaged their fields to pay taxes, fallen behind on their debts, and lost their land and vineyards. For this last group, the only remaining way to buy food is to sell their sons and daughters into slavery (vv. 4–5).

It is especially this third group that is being crushed. Taxes forced them to borrow. They pledged their land—the only means they had to feed themselves and earn money. When they could not repay, the lenders seized the pledged land. Now, with no way to grow food or earn income, they must sell their children to survive. Because they have no means of making money, they will likely never be able to redeem either their land or their children. That is why they cry out,

Now our flesh is as the flesh of our brothers, our children are as their children. Yet we are forcing our sons and our daughters to be slaves, and some of our daughters have already been enslaved, but it is not in our power to help it, for other men have our fields and our vineyards. (Neh 5:5)

Consider how heinous this oppression is. They say, “Our flesh is as the flesh of our brothers.” They are not selling their children to foreigners. They did not borrow from foreigners. They borrowed from and sold their children to other Jews. The loan sharks who seized their land were Jewish nobles. Rather than help their poor brothers and sisters through the crisis, they enriched themselves at their expense. Rather than relieve the crisis, they intensified it. This is one of the most extreme examples imaginable of putting your own interests above the interests of your own people. They had returned to rebuild Jerusalem together, and now they were seizing the very land they had just reclaimed. Seizing your brother’s property and buying his children so that they can eat is not exactly being a team player in the effort to rebuild the wall.

But it gets even worse. When Nehemiah accuses the nobles in verse 7, he reveals that he has been working to buy back Jewish brothers who had been sold to foreigners. Yet other Jewish nobles were selling their own brothers into slavery, and then Nehemiah and the upright nobles had to buy them back again—while the wicked nobles turned a profit in between.

A few other translations make this clearer:

“We are doing all we can to redeem our Jewish relatives who have had to sell themselves to pagan foreigners, but you are selling them back into slavery again. How often must we redeem them?” (NLT)

“We have done our best to buy back our Jewish countrymen who were sold to foreigners, but now you sell your own countrymen, and we have to buy them back.” (CSB)

Imagine the perspective of one of these Jewish nobles. A poor neighbor who has been away building the wall asks for a loan because his fields have not produced enough food. You accept his land as collateral. He buys grain—at famine prices—hoping for a late harvest. It never comes. Unable to repay, he loses the land. Now he has no way to grow food or earn money, so he offers to sell himself or his children. Some nobles accept them as slaves. Others allow them to be sold to foreigners, knowing Nehemiah will redeem them—and they pocket the profit.

This is exactly what God was protecting his people from when he forbade usury.

What Does the Old Testament Say about Usury?

Usury in the Old Testament refers to the regulations that forbid Israelites from charging one another interest. These commands are consistently paired with instructions about pledges and the general treatment of the poor.

Giving and taking loans is so ordinary in our society that interest-free loans seem almost unimaginable. But that is because credit is so easily available. We buy cars, homes, groceries, entertainment, and business investments—almost everything—on credit. The loans envisioned in the Mosaic law, however, are meant to help a poor brother through a tight spot. They are not about one wealthy person borrowing from another to launch a business.

Let me show you how clear the Old Testament regulations were, so that we can feel the weight of how godless and high-handed these Jewish nobles acted.

Exodus 22:25 says,

If you lend money to any of my people with you who is poor, you shall not be like a moneylender to him, and you shall not exact interest from him. If ever you take your neighbor’s cloak in pledge, you shall return it to him before the sun goes down, for that is his only covering, and it is his cloak for his body; in what else shall he sleep? And if he cries to me, I will hear, for I am compassionate.

God explicitly prohibits charging interest—the very thing the nobles did. He also forbids taking unreasonable pledges. If you are not permitted to keep your neighbor’s cloak overnight, do you really think you are permitted to keep his children or seize his land forever?

Leviticus 25:35–37 says,

“If your brother becomes poor and cannot maintain himself with you, you shall support him as though he were a stranger and a sojourner, and he shall live with you. Take no interest from him or profit, but fear your God, that your brother may live beside you. You shall not lend him your money at interest, nor give him your food for profit.”

Here God reveals the goal: to support the poor so that they can continue living as members of the covenant community. Out of the fear of God, the rich were to lend without interest so that their brother could remain among them rather than be driven out to survive. If you are commanded to keep him with you, you certainly must not sell him into slavery outside the covenant community.

God’s expectation is that those who have means will care for the poor and needy. After all, they had all been slaves in Egypt when God redeemed them at no cost to them.

Yet God’s people did not obey. Ezekiel 22:12 tells us that usury was one of the reasons God sent them into exile. In that chapter the Lord lists many heinous sins—political violence, abusing orphans and widows, profaning the Sabbath, every kind of sexual immorality including incest, taking bribes—and then declares,

You take interest and profit and make gain of your neighbors by extortion; but me you have forgotten, declares the Lord GOD… I will scatter you among the nations and disperse you through the countries, and I will consume your uncleanness out of you, and you shall be profaned by your own doing in the sight of the nations, and shall know that I am the Lord. (Ezek 22:12, 15–16)

This abuse of the poor is so serious in God’s sight that Psalm 15 declares (and we sing it),

Who shall abide in your temple, Lord? And who shall reside in your holy hill? … He won’t charge interest, nor will he take bribes. He who does this shall never be moved.

In other words, the one who cares for the poor the way God commands—by lending without interest—is the one welcomed into God’s presence. And by the same logic, the one who does charge interest had better not climb God’s holy hill, because God is furious with him. In fact, God promises that such a person will not keep his profit. Proverbs 28:8 says,

Whoever multiplies his wealth by interest and profit gathers it for him who is generous to the poor.

Exhortation: Beware the Love of Money. Be Generous.

The problem with these Jewish nobles is that they refused to put the interests of others above their own. They profited from the pain of God’s household. After all God had done for his people, they should have been willing to give generously to those in need.

Recall Jesus’ parable in Matthew 18:23–35, the parable of the unforgiving servant. A king brings before him a man with a massive debt. Unable to pay, the servant is ordered to be sold along with his wife and children. The man begs for patience, and the king, moved with pity, forgives the entire debt. But the servant immediately goes out and begins choking a fellow servant who owes him a small amount. When that man cannot pay, the forgiven servant throws him into prison. The king summons him back and throws him into jail because he refused to show the same mercy he had received.

The very fact that these Jewish nobles are back in the land at all is astonishing mercy. God had sent them into exile for refusing to fear him and walk in his ways. Yet in lovingkindness he had called them back according to his promise. He had forgiven their iniquity rather than hold it against them forever. They themselves should still be slaves to foreigners. And how do they respond? By charging interest on loans to their brothers, seizing their fields and vineyards, and forcing their children into slavery.

Beware, church. This is what the love of money will do to you. Jesus told us,

No one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and money.

Guard your heart against the love of money. If it takes root, you will find yourself doing exactly what these wicked nobles did—prioritizing selfish profit over the people God calls you to love and care for.

Brothers and sisters, may this selfish attitude never infect our church. In a world filled with progressive ideas that seek to seize wealth from some in order to redistribute it to others (while padding the pockets of those in charge), it would be easy for us to tighten our fists around what we have. Our rightful anger at foolish political policies could harden into animosity toward the poor and needy. Right now, those who are politically set against God’s good design for the household would love nothing more than for us to become a grumpy band of miserly complainers. They want to portray us as selfish, greedy capitalists who care only about the bottom line. Do not fall into their trap. Let us be a church that proves the caricature wrong—a community that shows that conservatives can be radically charitable. Let us give with the heart of the God who gave us life and breath, who fed us and let us enjoy sunrises and cool breezes for years before we ever knew him, the God who forgave us while we were still sinners dead set against him.

Some of you are on the poorer side of things. Your budget feels tight and necessities do not always feel secure. That does not mean you can opt out of living generously. After all, you are called to imitate the God who gave his only Son for you. Do not make excuses or push off generosity. Jesus warns,

For to everyone who has will more be given, and he will have an abundance. But from the one who has not, even what he has will be taken away. (Matthew 25:29)

Others of you are on the richer side. God has blessed you, and you have nothing to be ashamed of. Do not apologize for your wealth, and do not hoard it like a dragon sitting on its treasure. Paul commands you,

As for the rich in this present age, charge them not to be haughty nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches—but on God who richly provides us with everything to enjoy. They are to do good, be rich in good works, to be generous and ready to share, thus storing up treasure for themselves as a good foundation for the future, so that they may take hold of that which is truly life. (1 Tim 6:17–19)

God has forgiven all of us a debt we could never repay. Therefore let us be generous, loving one another even as he first loved us.

5:6–13 — Nehemiah Risks His Position and Spends Greatly to Rescue the People from Wicked Rulers

Now that Nehemiah knows about this injustice, all eyes are on him. What will he do? It would be tempting to look the other way. Confronting the nobles will mean standing against the very leaders whose support he needs to keep the project alive. He has already faced pressure from foreign leaders and from within Israel. How can he continue if the nobles themselves turn against him?

Rather than fear the consequences of angering the Jewish nobles, Nehemiah does what is right. He risks his position and spends greatly to rescue the people from their wicked rulers.

How Does Nehemiah Risk His Position?

Nehemiah risks his position by confronting the nobles publicly.

This pressure makes his courage all the more commendable. He puts himself on the line to do what is right and care for the people under his charge. God has made him governor, and he intends to shepherd them as best he can, no matter the cost. By the end of the chapter we will see that the cost is high.

Oh, for more political leaders like Nehemiah—leaders who will stand for what is right no matter what it costs them personally.

Wise man that he is, Nehemiah does not rush in. Verse 7 tells us that “he took counsel with himself”—he considered the matter carefully. His plan is to bring formal charges against the nobles, call them into a public trial, and settle the matter once and for all. It is not clear exactly who is invited to the “great assembly,” but it is certainly more than just Nehemiah and the nobles. He handles this public injustice in public.

Some of you may be thinking of Matthew 18 and Jesus’ instruction to confront a brother privately first, then with witnesses. Should Nehemiah have done that rather than call a public assembly? The nobles have sinned publicly against the whole community, and therefore their sin is addressed publicly. Matthew 18 addresses personal offenses between individuals. What we see here is Nehemiah doing what Paul would later command in Ephesians 5:11—exposing the unfruitful works of darkness by bringing them into the light. Nehemiah 5:7 suggests he first tried to settle the matter privately; when that failed, he called the great assembly. At the assembly he makes his case. There is no record of any counterargument. The community appears to recognize the injustice immediately and accepts the need for remedy.

How Does Nehemiah Spend Greatly?

He spends by forgiving the debts owed to him and by modeling the very generosity he demands of others.

Nehemiah calls for what amounts to a year of Jubilee: all debts forgiven, land returned, and even interest repaid. He stands up first and absorbs the cost himself. This is not an admission that he had been guilty of usury. The loans he had made were fair, but in this crisis even fair loans were crushing the people. He could not have been participating in the seizure of lands, or he would have already known about the oppression. In addition to forgiving his own loans, Nehemiah had been spending his own money to buy Jews back from slavery to foreigners. He refused the food allowance that was his by right as governor, yet he still kept a generous table for others. He employed his own servants in the work on the wall. All of this came after he had left a comfortable position as cupbearer in Susa.

This is what “my life for yours” looks like. Nehemiah considers the interests of others before his own. He lays down what he could rightfully claim. He uses his authority to serve rather than for selfish gain. That is real servant leadership. When leaders fail to take responsibility, it is not because they are too servant-hearted; it is because they have decided not to lead at all.

The Good Shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. Servant leadership has sometimes been twisted to mean the abdication of authority. Nehemiah does not divest himself of authority; he uses his authority to serve. In this chapter he places himself in a highly vulnerable position by confronting the nobles, yet he does what is right anyway. He is the opposite of the wicked rulers who put themselves first. He sacrifices reputation, political power, and wealth for the welfare of God’s people.

Exhortation: Imitate Nehemiah

If we are going to imitate Nehemiah who laid down his life, we must imitate not only his actions but his motivations. So why did he sacrifice so much on behalf of the people?

5:14–19 — Nehemiah Sacrificed So Much Because He Feared God, Not Man

Is Nehemiah Being Prideful?

Some wonder whether this section sounds self-serving. Nehemiah simply tells us his motives; we should not read evil into them. Throughout the book he is presented as a consistent example for us. Just as we did not assume his imprecatory prayer in chapter 4 was wrong, we should not assume this defense is wrong. If you were in his position, you too would want to prove that you had not mistreated the people, and you would bring the receipts. That is all Nehemiah is doing. It is prudent, not proud.

Nehemiah Is a Good Shepherd Because He Fears God

Nehemiah is not driven by a generic humanitarian impulse. Many unbelievers want to dig wells and relieve suffering. Nehemiah’s sacrifice flows from the fear of God (see Neh 5:19). The fear of God is what he appeals to when he rebukes the nobles: “Ought you not to walk in the fear of our God?” (5:9).

The Fear of God Makes Us Good Shepherds Because It Is…

A sword against our sin. The fear of God cuts through self-justification. It is the very motivation Nehemiah uses to confront the nobles’ mistreatment of God’s people.

A shield against compromise. The fear of God protected Nehemiah from being steered by friends or enemies alike. In chapter 1 he approached the king despite the danger. In chapter 2 the threats of Sanballat and Tobiah did not discourage him. In chapter 4 the opposition of some fellow Jews did not stop him. And here in chapter 5 the powerful Jewish nobles do not intimidate him. It was dangerous to confront them, yet he did what was right. Pastor Andy has been a good example of this same courage—standing for truth before God rather than fearing progressive opinion. Do you fear God more than your outspoken coworker, your strong-willed family member, or the social discomfort of being politically incorrect? The fear of God will keep you from compromise and from living by lies.

Fuel for sacrifice. The fear of God is what enables us to sing with sincerity, “Take the world, but give me Jesus,” or “Take my life and let it be consecrated, Lord, to thee.” It is the fuel that lets us lay down our rights and our resources for the good of others and the glory of God.
